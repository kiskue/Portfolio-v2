# Name the work, ask before copying it

**Yes, you can show that you built these apps, but you cannot freely copy the company's assets to prove it.** Under Philippine law and under US, EU and UK norms, you may say in plain words where you worked, what you built and which apps and LGU clients the work was for. You may also link to the public Google Play and App Store listings. Every claim must be true, and nothing may suggest that DGSI or the LGUs endorse you. Copies are a different matter. Store screenshots, app icons, logos, re-created screens that reuse DGSI's design, internal architecture and unpublished numbers need **written permission**, for two reasons. The Intellectual Property Code gives DGSI the copyright in work you did as part of your regular job, and the Supreme Court has said that commercial use weighs against fair use. Your site sells services, so it counts as commercial. For LGU apps, the government's standard contracts can make the software the LGU's property, so a "yes" from DGSI alone may not be enough. Some things are best left out even if your intentions are good: government seals and city logos, the privately distributed Responder app and the R&D prototype, security and operations details of an emergency system, and photos of real people. Your portfolio is not public yet. But the switch that makes it public removes none of the company, LGU or third-party material. Five red items must be fixed before you flip it, and a sixth before the repository itself is ever made public. This is general legal information, not legal advice.

## Philippine copyright gives DGSI the screens and you the credit

**Copyright** is the right to copy, change, show and share a creative work. In the Philippines it starts automatically. The IP Code protects works "from the moment of their creation". Its list expressly includes **computer programs**, drawings and technical illustrations ([RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)). That covers your code, the UI artwork, app icons, store screenshots and diagrams. Whoever owns the copyright holds the **economic rights**: to reproduce, adapt, publicly display and communicate the work. Since 2013, communicating the work expressly includes putting it online ([RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html)).

**DGSI owns what you made on the job.** Sec. 178.3 gives the copyright to the employer when "the work is the result of the performance of his regularly-assigned duties, unless there is an agreement, express or implied, to the contrary". Work outside your duties stays yours "even if the employee uses the time, facilities and materials of the employer" ([RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)). So the Defensys apps and Eventbook belong to DGSI by default, whether or not you signed a contract. SME Panindio, if you built it outside your DGSI duties, is yours. Freelance work follows a different rule. The paying client owns the delivered work, but "the copyright thereto shall remain with the creator, unless there is a written stipulation to the contrary" (Sec. 178.4). Since 2013, copyright "is not deemed assigned or licensed ... unless there is a written indication of such intention" (Sec. 180.2, [RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html)). In practice, a manager saying "sure, go ahead" out loud is weak proof. An email is strong, because an electronic document meets a legal writing requirement when it is reliable and can be authenticated ([RA 8792](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2000/ra_8792_2000.html)). One caution about the case law. The Supreme Court described these rules in *Republic v. Heirs of Tupaz* (2020), but it decided that case under the older law, P.D. 49. No Supreme Court ruling has applied Secs. 178.3 or 178.4 directly yet ([SC E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67242)). The statute's text is clear without one.

**The LGU may be a second owner.** In May 2025 the government issued a standard contract for consulting services, for use by national agencies and LGUs. It says "All computer programs developed by the Consultant under this Contract shall be the sole and exclusive property of the Procuring Entity". The consultant may use them only "with prior written approval of the Procuring Entity" (GCC 38.2). Another clause bars sharing non-public information without the agency's "prior written consent" (GCC 17) ([PBD for Consulting Services, 2025](https://www.bswm.da.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/NGPA_PBDs_Provision-for-Consultancy-Services-for-the-Development-of-a-Centralized-BSWM-Decision-Support-Hub_ADSigned.pdf)). The 2016 edition used the same words ([PBD, 5th ed.](https://www.upou.edu.ph/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/REINO21.pdf)). The New Government Procurement Act makes these standard forms mandatory for procuring entities ([RA 12009](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2024/ra_12009_2024.html)). If DGSI's LGU deals used them, DGSI itself needs the LGU's approval before it can let you show the apps. Only DGSI knows which contracts it signed. Software sold as goods, under a licence or through a partnership can come with different terms.

**You keep the right to be named.** Philippine law separates the owner from the author. The author is "the natural person who has created the work" (Sec. 171.1). The author keeps **moral rights**: personal rights that stay with the creator even after someone else owns the economic rights. They include the right "to require that the authorship of the works be attributed to him" (Sec. 193.1) ([RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)). Since 2013 this right lasts **forever**, and moral rights "shall not be assignable or subject to license" (Sec. 198.1, [RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html)). Two limits apply. The first is the **collective work**: a team product released under a company's name without individual credits. For a contribution to one, the right to demand credit "is deemed waived unless he expressly reserves it" (Sec. 196). The second is that authors can waive the right in writing (Sec. 195). Still, no waiver can let anyone use your name on "a work he did not create" (Sec. 195.2). So copyright never stops you from truthfully writing "I built the SOS flow in Defensys Citizen", because a fact copies nothing. But the right to be named is not a right to show the work, and showing it stays with DGSI. The same rule protects your teammates, so credit only the parts you built.

**Fair use is a weak shield for a site that sells services.** **Fair use** is a defence that allows some uses without permission. Sec. 185 covers "criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching ..., scholarship, research, and similar purposes". It weighs four factors, and the first asks "whether such use is of a commercial nature" ([RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html); [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)). In *ABS-CBN v. Gozon* (2015) the Court said that "commercial use of the copyrighted work can be weighed against fair use". It called fair use "a matter of defense" and ruled that good intentions do not help: "It is the act of infringement, not the intent, which causes the damage" ([ABS-CBN v. Gozon](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2015/mar2015/gr_195956_2015.html)). In 2022 the full Court went further: "Commercial use excludes fair use and should be weighed against it." It also refused to borrow US exemptions that are not in the IP Code, because doing so would be "legislating" ([FILSCAP v. Anrey, En Banc](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2022/aug2022/gr_233918_2022.html)). No court has ruled on portfolio screenshots, so the question is open, but do not base your plan on fair use. An owner who sues can choose **statutory damages**, a fixed award that needs no proof of loss, of at least **PHP 50,000** (cut to PHP 10,000 or less for an innocent infringer). A first criminal offence carries **1 to 3 years in prison and a fine of PHP 50,000 to 150,000** ([RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html); [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)).

**Ideas, data, names and links fall outside copyright; rebuilt screens do not.** Sec. 175 excludes any "idea, procedure, system, method or operation, concept, principle, discovery or mere data". A system architecture as such is therefore not a copyright problem, and neither are your metrics. Product names are trademarks, not copyright works ([Kho v. CA](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2002/mar2002/gr_115758_2002.html)). Their limits come from trademark and confidentiality law, covered below. Re-created screens are different. No Philippine case covers the "look and feel" of a user interface. Courts separate ideas from expression ([Joaquin v. Drilon](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1999/jan1999/gr_108946_1999.html)). They find infringement when "so much is taken that the value of the original is sensibly diminished" ([Habana v. Robles](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1999/jul1999/gr_131522_1999.html)). Reproduction covers copies made "in any manner or form", in whole or in part (Sec. 171.9, [RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html)). Rebuilding DGSI's screens in your own code still copies them if you reuse their wording, icons, design tokens and layout. Fictional data fixes privacy, not copyright. A screen that shows the same feature in your own visual style copies only the idea, and ideas are free. Links are safe. The EU's top court held that a link to a work freely available elsewhere reaches no "new public" and needs no permission ([Svensson](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/62012CJ0466)). A US appeals court held that linking without storing a copy is not a "display" ([Hunley v. Instagram](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2023/07/17/22-15293.pdf)). No Philippine case has dealt with links. Re-hosting a file on your own server, however, is plainly a new copy.

## Names are safe as words; logos, seals and badges signal endorsement

A **trademark** is a sign, such as a name, logo or app icon, that tells buyers who a product comes from. Every Philippine trademark claim turns on **confusion or deception**. Infringement needs use "in commerce" that "is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive" (Sec. 155). **Unfair competition** needs real deceit: "Deception, passing off and fraud upon the public are still the key elements" ([Coca-Cola v. Gomez](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2008/nov2008/gr_154491_2008.html)). The claim that fits a portfolio best is **false association** (Sec. 169.1(a)). It covers using a "name, symbol, or device" in a way likely to confuse people about "the affiliation, connection, or association" between you and someone else. It also covers confusion about "the origin, sponsorship, or approval" of your services ([RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)). It brings civil damages, and Sec. 170 also makes it a crime punishable by **2 to 5 years in prison and a fine of PHP 50,000 to 200,000**. A 2008 amendment to the core trademark right added only an exception for medicines ([RA 9502](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2008/ra_9502_2008.html)).

Philippine law has no written rule on **referential use**: using someone's brand name only to point at their product. No court has ruled on résumés or portfolios either. Foreign courts fill the gap. Their reasoning carries weight here because Sec. 169.1 copies the US Lanham Act almost word for word ([15 U.S.C. §1125](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1125)). The US test allows use of a mark if three things are true. The product is hard to identify without the mark. You use only as much of the mark as you need. You do nothing to "suggest sponsorship or endorsement" ([New Kids on the Block](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/971/971.F2d.302.90-56258.90-56219.html)). In a case about car brokers, the court agreed that using the stylized Lexus logo went too far: "The Tabaris could adequately communicate their message without using the visual trappings of the Lexus brand". The court added that a "not affiliated" disclaimer is "relevant" though "not required" ([Toyota v. Tabari](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf)). EU law allows referential use only "in accordance with honest practices", which rules out any impression of a "special relationship" ([Reg. 2017/1001](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/1001/oj); [BMW v. Deenik](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:61997CJ0063)). The common line is simple: **words that identify are fine; logos, icons and "Clients" or "Trusted by" framing borrow the owner's identity and suggest endorsement.** By this test, naming an LGU client in text is safe. The law requires every LGU contract to be posted at the provincial capitol or the city, municipal or barangay hall ([RA 7160](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1991/ra_7160_1991.html)). Taytay promotes its app on its own website, and the Google Play listing names DGSI as the developer ([Taytay](https://www.taytayrizal.gov.ph/single-post/smile-safe-taytay-app); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.dgsi.sagiptaytay&hl=en)).

**Government seals are the riskiest single item.** National law does not ban private display of an LGU seal. RA 8491's bans on commercial and advertising use cover the national flag. RA 7160 simply lets each LGU "have and use a corporate seal" ([RA 8491](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1998/ra_8491_1998.html); [RA 7160](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1991/ra_7160_1991.html)). The real limits are **local seal ordinances**. Batangas City makes it unlawful to post its seal "or replica thereof ... in or within any internet website, or social media platform" without a letter of authority from the Mayor. It grants those letters only for local non-profit, non-political purposes, and it fines offenders up to PHP 1,000, 2,000 and 3,000 ([Batangas City Ord. No. 22-06](https://www.batangascity.gov.ph/web/images/Ordinances2024/ORD20242206.pdf)). Its preamble explains why: people who see a seal "are likely to believe" that whoever displays it is official or approved. Each ordinance binds only its own city. Taytay's seal page lists no usage rules ([Taytay seal](https://www.taytayrizal.gov.ph/seal)). The rules of San Jose del Monte, Isabela City, Abra de Ilog and Bagong Pag-asa were not checked. Copyright adds a second layer. A seal drawn by LGU staff has no copyright, but using it "for profit" needs that office's "prior approval". A seal drawn by a private artist can be a copyright that the LGU owns (Secs. 176.1 and 176.3, [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)). At the far end, the Revised Penal Code punishes anyone who "shall knowingly and falsely represent himself to be an officer, agent or representative" of a government agency ([RA 379](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1949/ra_379_1949.html)). Never call yourself an LGU's "official developer" or "partner".

**Store badges belong to the app's publisher.** Apple licenses its badge "only in connection with Your applications that are available for download on the App Store", and only while "You" are a member of its developer program ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/marketing/guidelines/)). "You" means the account holder, which is DGSI or the LGU, not you. Google offers its badges for marketing "to promote content on Google Play" ([Google](https://partnermarketinghub.withgoogle.com/brands/google-play/google-play/lockups-icons-badges/#badges)). Its FAQ lists a few uses that need no approval, such as badges in small campaigns. When it answers what does need permission, the answer is "Everything else" ([Google FAQ](https://partnermarketinghub.withgoogle.com/support/faq/)). Google's 2023 rules also required every online badge to link to the store. They required the line "Google Play and the Google Play logo are trademarks of Google LLC." ([Google, 2023](http://web.archive.org/web/20230619010724/https://play.google.com/intl/en_us/badges/)). Plain text links avoid all of this. Apple's own credit line reads "App Store is a service mark of Apple Inc., registered in the U.S. and other countries." ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/marketing/guidelines/)).

**Technology logos come with brand rules too.** Your tool glyphs come from Simple Icons. The project disclaims responsibility and asks users to "seek the correct permissions" and follow each brand's guidelines ([Simple Icons](https://github.com/simple-icons/simple-icons/blob/develop/DISCLAIMER.md)). Expo allows its logo to show that you use Expo, but forbids "changing the color" ([Expo](https://expo.dev/brand)). Vercel, which owns Next.js, requires an attribution sentence and forbids modifying its marks. It also says "do not include Vercel marks on merchandise or marketing collateral for your commercial products or services" ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/design/brands)). React moved from Meta to the React Foundation under the Linux Foundation in February 2026 ([react.dev](https://react.dev/blog/2026/02/24/the-react-foundation)). The LF Projects trademark policy, which lists React, allows word marks "to make true factual statements". But it bars LF Projects logos on websites or marketing that promote your services without written permission ([LF Projects](https://lfprojects.org/policies/trademark-policy/)). Logo grids are common and the practical risk is low. Strict compliance, though, means text labels, or unmodified logos with a trademark line.

## Secrets, people's data and system details stay off-limits even without an NDA

An **NDA** (non-disclosure agreement) is a contract promise to keep information secret. Philippine law protects real secrets, personal data and some government information even where no NDA exists. A signed NDA only adds to that. Its definitions control, even for information that is not a strict trade secret, as long as they are reasonable and specific.

**Trade secrets.** The Philippines has no trade secrets act. The IP Code names "Protection of Undisclosed Information" as an IP right but builds no legal claim around it ([RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)). The Supreme Court defines a **trade secret** as information "known only to its owner and those of his employees to whom it is necessary to confide it". That can include a "compilation of information" or a "specialized customer list", and the Court calls trade secrets "proprietary rights" ([Air Philippines v. Pennswell](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2007/dec2007/gr_172835_2007.html)). An employer's label is not enough, though. When management calls something a secret, that view "must have a substantial factual basis which can pass judicial scrutiny". Techniques that were "readily available to the public" did not qualify ([Cocoland v. NLRC](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1996/jul1996/gr_98458_1996.html)). For software the line is clear. Internal architecture, security design, source code, unreleased features and unpublished numbers are likely secrets. Store listings, public screenshots and the LGUs' own announcements are not. Your general skills stay yours. The EU says its trade secrets law gives no ground for "limiting employees' use of experience and skills honestly acquired" ([Directive 2016/943](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2016/943/article/1)), and no Philippine case says otherwise.

**Criminal, civil and contract rules.** Revised Penal Code Art. 291 punishes "any manager, employee or servant who, in such capacity, shall learn the secrets of his principal or master and shall reveal such secrets". The penalty is now one month and one day to six months in jail, plus a fine of up to **PHP 100,000** ([RA 10951](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2017/ra_10951_2017.html); [RPC](https://lawphil.net/statutes/acts/act1930/act_3815_1930.html)). The research found no reported case applying it. Contracts "have the force of law between the contracting parties" (Civil Code Art. 1159). The Civil Code's unfair-competition action "covers even cases of discovery of trade secrets of a competitor" ([Civil Code](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1949/ra_386_1949.html); [Willaware v. Jesichris](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2014/sep2014/gr_195549_2014.html)). That matters if your Services page competes for DGSI's clients. A **non-compete clause** is valid if it has "reasonable limitations as to time, trade, and place" ([Tiu v. Platinum Plans](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2007/feb2007/gr_163512_2007.html)), and the employer must prove it is reasonable ([Rivera v. Solidbank](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2006/apr2006/gr_163269_2006.html)).

**Your job, while you still have it.** The Labor Code lets an employer dismiss for serious misconduct or for "willful breach ... of the trust reposed in him" (Art. 297, formerly Art. 282) ([Labor Code](https://lawphil.net/statutes/presdecs/pd1974/pd_442_1974.html); [Fabricator v. Estolas](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/4/63466)). The Supreme Court checks employers who overreach. One company's catch-all "confidential information" rule "suffers from vagueness", and a rank-and-file analyst could not be dismissed under it ([Yonzon v. Coca-Cola](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/4/67790)). A manager's Facebook post where "there was really no actual leakage of information" did not justify dismissal, because "A lighter penalty would have sufficed" ([Interadent v. Simbillo](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2016/nov2016/gr_207315_2016.html)). But a manager who e-mailed internal business information to a partner company lost her case. The Court held that "the mere existence of a basis for believing that a managerial employee has breached the trust of the employer justifies dismissal" ([Gana v. NLRC](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45313)). Publishing public material looks like *Simbillo*. Publishing internals looks like *Gana*. Leaving DGSI changes less than people think. Ownership was fixed when you made the work, and the privacy duty below survives. A confidentiality clause written to survive your exit probably still binds you, although no Supreme Court case has squarely ruled on one. Only the dismissal risk ends. Claims against a former employee go to the regular courts ([Dai-Chi v. Villarama](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/nov1994/gr_112940_1994.html)).

**Real people's data.** The Data Privacy Act covers any information "from which the identity of an individual is apparent or can be reasonably and directly ascertained" (Sec. 3(g)). It protects **sensitive personal information** more strictly. That includes information about "any proceeding for any offense committed or alleged to have been committed" ([RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html)). Re-posting a screenshot with an identifiable face is "processing" that needs a legal basis. For ordinary data, a "legitimate interest" can work if it passes a three-part test of purpose, necessity and balance ([NPC AO 2020-046](https://www.privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Redacted-Advisory-Opinion-No.-2020-046.pdf)). But self-promotion weighs little against a stranger's face. For sensitive data, consent is the only exception that could fit a portfolio. Fictional data is outside the law. In the NPC's words, "anonymized data, in its truest sense, is not considered as personal information" ([NPC AO 2017-027](https://privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/NPC_AdvisoryOpinionNo._2017-027.pdf)). Aggregate figures "cannot identify an individual" ([NPC AO 2019-036](https://privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/v1Redacted-Advisory-Opinion-No.-2019-036.pdf)). A screenshot with names and other identifiers "redacted or cropped out ... might not be within the scope of the DPA" ([NPC AO 2020-043](https://www.privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Redacted-Advisory-Opinion-No.-2020-043.pdf)). Staff who handle personal data must keep it confidential "even after ... termination of employment" (Sec. 20(e)). The implementing rules extend that duty to staff of companies that process data for others ([IRR Sec. 26(d)](https://privacy.gov.ph/implementing-rules-regulations-data-privacy-act-2012/)). Unauthorized disclosure by an employee carries **1 to 3 years in prison and a fine of PHP 500,000 to 1,000,000** (Sec. 32).

**System details.** No Philippine law forbids describing, at a high level, how an LGU app is built. The 1964 rules on classified matter bind government offices and cover only material that has actually been classified ([MC 78](https://lawphil.net/executive/mc/mc1964/mc_78_1964.html)). The 2024 order on the national cybersecurity plan imposes nothing on private persons ([EO 58](https://lawphil.net/executive/execord/eo2024/eo_58_2024.html)). The Cybercrime Act punishes sharing passwords or access codes with criminal intent, not drawing diagrams ([RA 10175](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10175_2012.html)). Security practice still draws a line. NIST says "System security should not depend on the secrecy of the implementation or its components" ([NIST SP 800-123](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-123.pdf)). Yet attackers search "freely available websites" for information about targets, including sites about "hiring or requested/rewarded contracts" ([MITRE ATT&CK T1593](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1593/)). OWASP lists "Exposure of Sensitive System Information" among the weaknesses behind its top web-application risk ([OWASP](https://top10.owasp.org/2021/A01_2021-Broken_Access_Control/index.html)). Accepting that risk is a decision for the system's owners, the LGUs and DGSI. It is not a decision for your portfolio.

## The US, EU and UK draw the same line, and so does industry practice

**Treaties set the floor.** The Berne Convention gives every author the right "to claim authorship of the work", "even after the transfer" of the economic rights ([Berne Art. 6bis](https://www.law.cornell.edu/treaties/berne/6bis.html)). Philippine Sec. 193 follows it. TRIPS requires protection for information that is secret, valuable because it is secret, and guarded by "reasonable steps". It treats "breach of contract, breach of confidence" as dishonest practices ([TRIPS Art. 39](https://www.wto.org/english/docs_e/legal_e/27-trips_04d_e.htm)).

**United States.** "A work prepared by an employee within the scope of his or her employment" is a **work made for hire**, and the employer is legally "the author" ([17 U.S.C. §101](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/101); [§201](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/201)). That is stricter than Philippine law, where you remain the author. US fair use weighs the same four factors ([§107](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/107)). American designers often hear that portfolio display "is fair use, regardless of who owns the copyright" and that owners "rarely object". That is one practitioner's 2004 opinion, and even she calls written agreements "the safest practice" ([Graphic Artists Guild](https://graphicartistsguild.org/fair-use-or-infringement/)). Courts are narrower. A trial court accepted a design firm's promotional photos that "only incidentally included" parts of a commissioned sculpture ([Neri v. Monroe](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/neri-monroe-7thcir2013.pdf)). A portfolio screenshot is different: it makes the employer's work the main content. Since 2023 the rule has been this: when a commercial use shares the original's purpose, "the first fair use factor is likely to weigh against fair use, absent some other justification for copying" ([Warhol v. Goldsmith](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/21-869)). Store screenshots are promotional images, and a portfolio that sells services is promotion too.

**European Union.** For software an employee writes "in the execution of his duties", the employer "exclusively shall be entitled to exercise all economic rights". "Computer programs" also include "their preparatory design material", so design documents count as part of the employer's program ([Directive 2009/24/EC](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32009L0024)). A screen design can be protected on its own if it is "its author's own intellectual creation". Parts whose expression is "dictated by their technical function" are not original ([BSA v. Ministerstvo kultury](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/62009CJ0393)). Linking to freely available work needs no permission ([Svensson](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/62012CJ0466)). But posting on your own site a photo that was freely available elsewhere is a new communication that needs the owner's consent ([Renckhoff](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62017CJ0161)). Presenting your product "as an imitation or replica" of a branded one is not an honest practice ([Gillette](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62003CJ0228)). The GDPR's household exemption covers only activity with "no connection to a professional or commercial activity" ([GDPR](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32016R0679)). Posting personal data online for "an indefinite number of people" is not private or family life ([Lindqvist](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/62001CJ0101)).

**United Kingdom.** The employer is first owner of works an employee makes "in the course of his employment" ([CDPA s.11](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/11)). The right to be identified as author "does not apply" to "a computer program" ([CDPA s.79](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/79)). On paper, then, a Philippine developer has stronger moral rights than a British or American one. Philippine fair use, meanwhile, is stricter about commercial use than US practice.

**Industry practice agrees.** The AIGA's model design contract gives freelance designers a portfolio right. Yet it makes each side's description of the project "subject to the other's reasonable approval". Its guide expects the work to be "introduced to the public" first. It also warns that clients in competitive fields may want to review promotion "on a case-by-case basis" ([AIGA](https://davidberman.com/wp-content/uploads/AIGA-Standard-Form-of-Agreement-for-Design-Services.pdf)). Common freelance templates now give the freelancer no rights "except those that the Client explicitly grants" ([Bonsai](https://www.hellobonsai.com/contract-template/freelance)). So for future clients, write a portfolio clause into the contract before work starts. Nielsen Norman Group says NDAs are common, "even more so if you're doing government work". It advises recreating designs "using different styles", without logos and brand colours. It also warns that future employers read "sloppy handling of sensitive data as a negative signal" ([NN/g 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/); [NN/g 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/maintain-ux-portfolio/)). Toptal advises anonymizing the client and omitting sensitive screens, while being "upfront about what's been withheld" ([Toptal](https://www.toptal.com/designers/ui/case-study-portfolio)). A password wall does not fix the problem: "Password protection does not bypass your legal NDA obligations" ([SlateGit](https://slategit.com/blog/developer-portfolio-confidential-work-nda)). A Philippine remote-staffing firm gives the same advice. Ask first, keep the request "professional and respectful", and do not assume a yes ([Remote Staff](https://www.remotestaff.ph/?p=17353)). When disputes happen they usually end with a removal request, and the standard advice is to "comply" ([Millo](https://millo.co/can-i-include-my-previous-in-house-work-in-my-portfolio)). The research found no reported court case over an ordinary portfolio.

## Conclusion

The useful line is not company work versus personal work. It is **words versus copies versus secrets**. Words about your role are yours to publish anywhere, as long as they are true, and Philippine law even makes your right to be named permanent. Copies of DGSI's screens, icons and designs belong to DGSI. After *FILSCAP v. Anrey*, a Philippine portfolio that sells services cannot lean on fair use the way American designers are often told they can. Secrets, security details and other people's faces should stay out, however good your intentions. For LGU work, one yes may not be enough, because the government's own contract forms make the software the LGU's property and require its prior written consent. Only DGSI knows which forms it signed, so the permission email is also the fastest way to find out who has to agree.

The second takeaway is more encouraging: the safe version of this portfolio is also the stronger one. Hiring managers want to see how you "started with an opportunity and produced real value for a user and the organization" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)). They do not need pixel-perfect copies of someone else's interface. Your role, your decisions, your results, links to live apps and replicas in your own style are exactly the material you can publish without anyone's permission. Nothing is indexed yet, so fixing the red items now costs a few commits. Fixing them after a takedown request would cost trust with the employer whose name is on your best work.
