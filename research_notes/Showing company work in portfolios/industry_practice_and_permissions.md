# Showing company work in portfolios: industry practice and permissions

Research date: 2026-10-23. Scope: professional norms for showing employer, client or NDA-covered work in a portfolio; disclaimers; Philippine perspectives; written permission requests; how disputes play out. This is research, not legal advice. Legend: **fetched** = I opened the page and the quote comes from it; **search summary only** = seen only through a search-engine summary, page not opened, treat as unverified; **opinion** = practitioner or vendor opinion piece; **pre-2018** = older than the preferred window. Statute text is the Philippine IP Code (RA 8293) as shown on lawphil.net (the 1997 text); I did not check whether RA 10372 (2013) touched §§155, 178, 193 or 195.

## Q1. What do professional bodies and contract templates say (AIGA, Graphic Artists Guild, freelance "portfolio rights" clauses, Upwork/Toptal)?

### Takeaway
Designer-side model terms (AIGA) give the independent designer a portfolio and self-promotion right, but how each side describes the relationship stays "subject to the other's reasonable approval", and AIGA's own guide warns that clients in competitive fields often want to review each use. Current generic freelance templates (Bonsai, as fetched) do the opposite by default: once paid, the freelancer has no rights unless the client grants them, so portfolio rights have to be written into the contract. None of these models covers employees. Work an employee does as part of regular duties belongs to the employer by default (Philippine IP Code §178.3(b)), so for employees the norm is to ask permission.

### Cited Findings
**AIGA (American professional association for design; model contract, not law)**
- AIGA Standard Form of Agreement for Design Services, Basic Terms and Conditions §6 "Accreditation/Promotions" (the copy I opened says "© 2009 AIGA First edition published in 2001"; fetched, text extracted from the PDF): "All displays or publications of the Deliverables shall bear accreditation and/or copyright notice in Designer's name in the form, size and location as incorporated by Designer in the Deliverables, or as otherwise directed by Designer. Designer retains the right to reproduce, publish and display the Deliverables in Designer's portfolios and websites, and in galleries, design periodicals and other media or exhibits for the purposes of recognition of creative excellence or professional advancement, and to be credited with authorship of the Deliverables in connection with such uses. Either party, subject to the other's reasonable approval, may describe its role in relation to the Project and, if applicable, the services provided to the other party on its website and in other promotional materials, and, if not expressly objected to, include a link to the other party's website." — [AIGA Standard Form PDF (third-party copy hosted on davidberman.com)](https://davidberman.com/wp-content/uploads/AIGA-Standard-Form-of-Agreement-for-Design-Services.pdf). The official AIGA page ([aiga.org](https://aiga.org/resources/aiga-standard-form-of-agreement-for-design-services)) returned HTTP 403 when I fetched it. ### Inferences
- The AIGA and GAG portfolio rights depend on the creator being an independent contractor who has not assigned copyright, or has only licensed it. They do not carry over to employees. For an employee doing regular duties in the Philippines, IP Code §178.3(b) gives copyright to the employer (see key claims), so reproducing the employer's screenshots, icons, code or documents generally needs the employer's permission.
- For commissioned or contract work in the Philippines, IP Code §178.4 keeps copyright with the creator unless a written agreement says otherwise. That is closer to the AIGA model, but confidentiality clauses and any assignment in the contract can still rule out display.
- Generic templates now default to "no rights after payment", so portfolio rights need to be negotiated up front.
- Even the most designer-friendly model (AIGA) makes describing the client relationship "subject to the other's reasonable approval" and expects the designer to wait until the work is "introduced to the public". The professional norm is to coordinate with the client, not to display unilaterally.

## Q2. What do hiring managers and practice guides recommend for showing NDA or company work?

### Takeaway
The sources agree on a playbook: lead with your role, process, decisions and outcomes in your own words; remove or replace identifying details (logos, brand colours, names, real data); recreate work instead of copying it, ideally in a different visual style; point to public material and links; save deeper detail for interviews or calls; get written permission before showing real assets; and do not treat a password wall as a substitute for permission. Hiring managers mostly want process and impact, and sloppy handling of confidential material counts against you.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g, "5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio" (Rachel Krause, 4 Aug 2019; fetched): "Nondisclosure agreements are common when doing UX work — and even more so if you're doing government work." "These contracts prohibit you from displaying identifying information about the company, its users, or the project details." It recommends showing process (sketches, black-and-white wireframes) "Rather than showing the polished UI and visual designs that display company-specific information". It also advises: "If you have wireframes or prototypes that you'd like to show, blur out identifying information." "(Blurring is especially important for applications displaying financial or medical information.)" "Redacting personal information like names and contact information". "Recreate your designs using different styles." It notes this is time-consuming but keeps brand colours and styles that could identify the client out of the portfolio, and suggests removing logos, changing branding and updating copy. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- Same NN/g article: NN/g "surveyed 204 UX professionals in charge of hiring". One quoted hiring manager: "Show me how you started with an opportunity and produced real value for a user and the organization." Another: "Very rarely will hiring managers take the time to read your entire portfolio word for word". — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- NN/g, "How to Maintain a UX Portfolio Over Time" (Evan Sunwall, 15 Jan 2023; fetched): "Of course, always respect nondisclosure agreements, employment contracts, data-security policies, or privacy laws." "Some industries like finance, healthcare, or government-related projects have strict rules around some of these materials." "Be prepared to anonymize or redact materials to make them portfolio-ready." "Future employers perusing your portfolio may view your sloppy handling of sensitive data as a negative signal." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/maintain-ux-portfolio/)
- NN/g, "Portfolios for UX Researchers" (**search summary only**): research documents can sometimes be "sanitized" so the company or product can't be recognised; past clients may agree to short excerpts; a client testimonial can stand in for confidential work. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-researcher-portfolio/)
- SlateGit, "Developer Portfolio Confidential Work NDA" (SlateGit Team, 16 July 2026; vendor blog, **opinion**; fetched): "Do not copy and paste your employer's code." Instead, isolate the engineering problem and write a fresh clean-room implementation with a README saying it is a clean-room version of a pattern used at work. Diagram systems with vendor-neutral components. For metrics: "Instead of stating exact revenue figures, use percentages, system throughput scale, or resource utilization metrics." Describe the employer by scale and sector (e.g. "a mid-sized healthcare SaaS provider"). "Password protection does not bypass your legal NDA obligations if you are still displaying proprietary code or data." It says blurring logos in UI screenshots is usually not enough and recommends architecture diagrams and design write-ups instead. It claims recruiters "care more about your architectural decision-making than your ability to write boilerplate CRUD routes." Example rewrite: "Built the AlphaStream market-execution engine for Apex Capital." becomes "Designed a high-frequency, low-latency market data ingestion pipeline for an institutional financial services firm." — [SlateGit](https://slategit.com/blog/developer-portfolio-confidential-work-nda)
- Remote Staff, "How to Build A Portfolio Without Violating Your NDA" (Herschel Lili, 27 March 2024; Makati-based remote-staffing company writing for Filipino remote workers; **opinion**; fetched): read the NDA closely, since "NDAs expire after a certain period"; ask for permission and keep the request "professional and respectful", without assuming a yes (some may allow redacted versions); if permission is refused or the employer can't be reached, build mock-ups with invented company names; password-protect files and share them only with the hiring manager; add a short background note to each sensitive file and say at the start of the portfolio that you obtained permission; collect testimonials. — [Remote Staff](https://www.remotestaff.ph/?p=17353)
- TechWhirl, "Building an NDA-Compliant Portfolio" (Connie Giordano; dated about 2011 from its image path; **pre-2018**; fetched): "Get that permission in writing either in the NDA or in specific requests." "Be willing to scrub data, remove identifying trademarks, brands or color schemes." "Offer to redact portions as requested." "If needed, agree to wait for a period of time before including the sample in your physical or online portfolio." "Create a text summary of the work you did, without any kind of identifying information." — [TechWhirl](https://techwhirl.com/building-an-nda-compliant-portfolio/)
- Millo, "Can I Put Company Projects in my Own Portfolio?" (updated 29 Jan 2024; **opinion**, U.S. work-for-hire framing; fetched): "You can't legally show projects in your portfolio that you created as an employee without permission"; "Check your employment contract or company policies to see if there are any restrictions on sharing your work."; "Just ask for permission anyway." — [Millo](https://millo.co/can-i-include-my-previous-in-house-work-in-my-portfolio)
- UX Collective / Bootcamp posts (**search summaries only**; Medium, partly paywalled; **opinion**):
  - Redaction is the most common approach.
  - A password adds friction that can put recruiters off.
  - Use fake data, such as a sample heat map. — [uxdesign.cc "Is NDA getting in the way of your design career?"](https://uxdesign.cc/is-nda-in-the-way-of-your-design-career-5-ways-to-navigate-around-it-215176bea567)
  - Replace the logo with a fictitious brand name, and avoid charts and numbers rather than inventing figures.
  - One designer rebuilt an NDA project from scratch and showed it only on a one-to-one call. — [bootcamp.uxdesign.cc "Sshhh…! It's an NDA project"](https://bootcamp.uxdesign.cc/sshhh-its-an-nda-project-ef7fe1af75cc)
  - Explain the basics so the recruiter wants more, then go deeper in interviews.
  - Check the product's marketing site and the live product (as a "customer") for visuals you can use. — [uxdesign.cc "How to add an NDA project to your portfolio"](https://uxdesign.cc/how-to-add-an-nda-project-to-your-portfolio-d2932684be01)
  - A password-protected page is a turn-off for visitors. — [uxdesign.cc best portfolios](https://uxdesign.cc/the-best-ux-designer-portfolios-inspiring-case-studies-and-examples-7402e6704923)
- YunoJuno (**search summary only**): describe the client by its market position rather than by name. — [YunoJuno](https://yunojuno.com/blogs/3-great-things-to-include-in-your-yunojuno-portfolio-even-if-youre-working-under-nda)
- Apple App Store marketing guidelines (fetched), on screenshots: "you should display fictional account information instead of data from a real person." — [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/marketing/guidelines/)
- **Disagreement on password protection:** Remote Staff recommends it ([Remote Staff](https://www.remotestaff.ph/?p=17353)). UX Collective writers call it friction or a turn-off (search summaries above). SlateGit says it does not cure an NDA breach ([SlateGit](https://slategit.com/blog/developer-portfolio-confidential-work-nda)).

### Inferences
These inferences apply the playbook to the asker's items. Risk tiers are my synthesis of the sources above.
- **Generally accepted with no assets** (the description-only pattern every guide endorses):
  - Your role, responsibilities, decisions and outcomes in your own words.
  - Your tech stack.
  - Your employer and job title as they appear on a CV or LinkedIn.
  - Plain-text links to public store listings, which point to the owner's own page instead of copying it.
  - The FAQ line that company code is proprietary.
- **Commonly allowed only with permission or conditions:**
  - Re-hosted official store screenshots and app icons: this copies assets owned by the employer or the LGU and carries their marks. Guides prefer linking to the listing or getting written permission.
  - "Replica" screens with fictional data that keep LGU names and brand colours: NN/g's advice is to change styles, logos and copy so the client can't be identified. Exact replicas still identify the client and may count as derivative of the employer's UI.
  - Exact metrics: public figures (such as store install ranges) are lower risk if sourced; internal numbers need approval, or can be given as relative figures.
  - System architecture diagrams: keep them high-level and vendor-neutral, with no hostnames, security controls or data flows that expose attack surface.
- **Usually objected to, or to avoid without explicit permission:**
  - Screenshots or details of the privately distributed responder app, which is not public.
  - The R&D push-to-talk prototype, which is unreleased: wait for launch or permission.
  - Any code or internal documents.
  - Security-relevant details of public-safety systems: NN/g flags government work as stricter.
  - Real citizen data.
  - Official LGU seals and emblems: their legal status was not researched here.
- Naming which colleague built each part of the system is a courtesy issue as well as a confidentiality one. Many guides stress being exact about your own role (SlateGit), but naming others may need their consent.

### Gaps
- I found no published survey of engineering hiring managers on NDA work. The NN/g survey covers UX hiring managers, and SlateGit is vendor opinion. I did not find a Smashing Magazine piece on this from 2018 or later; its portfolio pieces I saw date from 2009.

## Q3. How do agencies and developers word disclaimers?

### Takeaway
Four wording patterns recur:
1. A notice that trademarks belong to their owners and their use does not imply endorsement.
2. A "shown with permission" note.
3. A note that screens were re-created with fictional data and are not the real product.
4. Platform credit lines, such as Apple's formats.

App-store badges are licensed for promoting your own published apps (Apple says so explicitly), so a developer linking to apps published by an employer or client should use plain text links. Disclaimers do not cure a copyright or confidentiality breach (my inference); their job is to prevent an implied endorsement.

### Cited Findings

- Magic UI "social proof press" docs (**search summary only**): the logos shown belong to others and appear only for illustrative and educational purposes, with no implied sponsorship; companies are invited to request removal. — [Magic UI](https://magicui.design/docs/sections/social-proof-press)

  - "When the App Store badge is used, credit both Apple and the Apple Logo."
  - "Include the credit lines only once in your communication or website, and place the credit lines wherever you provide legal notification."
  - "Do not use trademark symbols on products, product documentation, or other product communications that will be distributed outside the United States."

### Inferences
These templates are drafted from the patterns above and are not quoted from any source:
- "I worked on [App] as [role] at [Employer]. [App] is published for [LGU]. Names, logos and marks belong to their respective owners; their appearance here does not imply endorsement of me or my services."
- "Screens on this page are my own re-creations using fictional data. They are not the production app and contain no real user information."
- "Shown with the written permission of [Employer], [date]." Use this only when permission was actually given.
- "Source code, internal systems and non-public apps are proprietary to [Employer] and are not shown."
- "Technology logos indicate tools I use; they are trademarks of their owners and do not imply endorsement."
- Because the Services page makes the site commercial, a no-endorsement line matters more there. Apple's licence is limited to "Your applications", so for apps you don't publish, a plain text link such as "View on Google Play" or "View on the App Store" is safer than the official badges.

## Q4. What do Philippine employers and the Philippine tech community typically allow or expect?

### Takeaway
I found very little published Philippine-specific guidance. What exists, a Remote Staff article (2024) and a Respicio & Co. commentary (2025), repeats the global playbook: check the contract and NDA, ask permission, sanitise or re-create. The legal background points the same way: the employer owns regular-duty works, confidentiality survives resignation, and labour tribunals require a wilful breach and a proportionate penalty before dismissal (Interadent v. Simbillo). I found no Philippine survey of company portfolio, LinkedIn or GitHub policies, and no community thread.

### Cited Findings
- IP Code §178.3: "In the case of work created by an author during and in the course of his employment, the copyright shall belong to:"
  - (a) "The employee, if the creation of the object of copyright is not a part of his regular duties". This holds even if the employee used the employer's time, facilities or materials (paraphrase).
  - (b) "The employer, if the work is the result of the performance of his regularly-assigned duties," "unless there is an agreement, express or implied, to the contrary."
  
  — [RA 8293 on lawphil](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- IP Code §178.4: "In the case of a work commissioned by a person other than an employer of the author and who pays for it" … "the person who so commissioned the work shall have ownership of the work," but "the copyright thereto shall remain with the creator, unless there is a written stipulation to the contrary;" — [RA 8293 on lawphil](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- IP Code §193.1 (moral rights, independent of economic rights): "To require that the authorship of the works be attributed to him," including "the right that his name, as far as practicable, be indicated in a prominent way on the copies". §195: "An author may waive his rights mentioned in Section 193 by a written instrument," with limits. — [RA 8293 on lawphil](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Respicio & Co., "Ownership of Employee-Developed 'Work Formats' After Resignation" (Harold Respicio, 3 July 2025; law-firm commentary marked educational; fetched):
  - "Employer owns economic rights if creation is part of regular duties unless a contract says otherwise."
  - "the departed employee cannot exploit or sell the same format", but the employee "retains moral rights unless waived".
  - "Even without a non-compete, using or disclosing a former employer's trade secret is actionable (injunction + damages)."
  - An NDA "Survives beyond separation; typical carve-out for information in public domain."
  - Employees should "Keep private projects separate (devices, accounts, off-hours)" and "Document personal contributions".
  
  — [Respicio & Co.](https://www.respicio.ph/commentaries/ownership-of-employee-developed-work-formats-after-resignation)
- Respicio search results (**search summary only**): displaying company work publicly can count as reproduction or communication to the public, which needs the owner's permission; samples containing personal or client data raise separate privacy issues; a common approach is to ask the employer for written permission to show sanitised or approved samples. — [Respicio commentaries](https://www.respicio.ph/commentaries/economic-and-moral-rights-conferred-by-philippine-copyright-law)
- Respicio, "Multiple Employment in the Philippine Private Sector" (**search summary only**): Philippine employment contracts often cover confidentiality, intellectual property, use of company resources and outside work; exclusivity clauses may be upheld if reasonable. — [Respicio](https://www.respicio.ph/commentaries/multiple-employment-in-the-philippine-private-sector)
- Rivermate (**search summary only**): confidentiality clauses are generally enforceable in the Philippines if the information is genuinely confidential and the scope is reasonable. — [Rivermate](https://rivermate.com/guides/philippines/agreements)
- Remote Staff (Philippine company; 2024): general playbook covering NDA expiry, permission, mock-ups, password protection and disclaimers. The fetch confirmed it cites no Philippine law. — [Remote Staff](https://www.remotestaff.ph/?p=17353)
- *Interadent Zahntechnik Philippines, Inc. v. Simbillo*, G.R. No. 207315, 23 Nov 2016 (Second Division, Del Castillo, J.; fetched):
  - An employee was dismissed for loss of trust over a Facebook post. The Court held: "No company information or corporate record was divulged by Simbillo."
  - On the standard: "to be a valid ground, loss of trust and confidence must be based on willful breach of trust." Acts done "carelessly, thoughtlessly, heedlessly, or inadvertently" do not qualify.
  - On the penalty: "A lighter penalty would have sufficed for Simbillo's laxity and carelessness."
  - The Labor Arbiter and NLRC had upheld the dismissal; the CA and the Supreme Court found it illegal and awarded separation pay and backwages.
  
  — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2016/nov2016/gr_207315_2016.html)
- NN/g says NDAs are common "even more so if you're doing government work" ([NN/g 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)) and that "government-related projects have strict rules" ([NN/g 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/maintain-ux-portfolio/)). This matters because DGSI's clients are LGUs.
- Community: no r/phcareers thread on the topic came up. The only result was a page describing the subreddit. — [GummySearch r/phcareers](https://gummysearch.com/r/phcareers/)

### Inferences
- Philippine law makes the employer the default copyright owner of regular-duty work. A Philippine employee who wants to show screenshots, icons, code or documents from DGSI work therefore needs permission, unless the work was outside regular duties (§178.3(a)) or the contract says otherwise.
- The moral right of attribution (§193.1) supports truthfully claiming authorship ("I built the incident-reporting module"). It does not give a right to copy the employer's assets. That distinction is my inference.
- DGSI's apps are published under LGU brands, and DGSI's contracts with the LGUs probably contain their own confidentiality and publicity terms. DGSI may not be able to approve showing LGU-branded material without the LGU's consent (inference; contracts unknown).
- Interadent suggests Philippine tribunals ask whether actual confidential information was disclosed and whether the penalty was proportionate. Public store-listing information is probably not "confidential", which fits the public-domain carve-outs in AIGA and Respicio. But copying copyrighted assets is a separate copyright question from confidentiality.

## Q5. What should a written permission request cover, and how do companies usually respond?

### Takeaway
Ask in writing, list each item separately, say what it is for, present it as good for the company, and offer safeguards: review before publishing, redaction, waiting until launch, a credit line, and takedown on request. AIGA's guide says competitive clients typically want case-by-case review. Anecdotes and guides say employers often say yes with conditions, sometimes generously. If they refuse or remove permission, comply and fall back to text-only or re-created samples. I found no data on how often requests are approved.

### Cited Findings
- AIGA guide: items worth securing are a credit line, the client's name on your client list, entering the work in design competitions, and showing and explaining portions of the work to prospective clients, all "once the project has been completed and introduced to the public". Competitive clients "may ask for the right to review and approve such promotional activity on a case-by-case basis." — [AIGA PDF](https://davidberman.com/wp-content/uploads/AIGA-Standard-Form-of-Agreement-for-Design-Services.pdf)
- AIGA §6: each party "subject to the other's reasonable approval, may describe its role" and, "if not expressly objected to, include a link to the other party's website." — [AIGA PDF](https://davidberman.com/wp-content/uploads/AIGA-Standard-Form-of-Agreement-for-Design-Services.pdf)
- TechWhirl (pre-2018):
  - "Determine if you have permission to use samples and how much you can use."
  - "Get that permission in writing either in the NDA or in specific requests."
  - Suggested framing: "I really enjoy my work here, and would love to be able to demonstrate what I've done."
  - "Show that you are on their side, be selective about which projects and samples you are requesting."
  - Offer something in return; offer to scrub, redact or wait.
  
  — [TechWhirl](https://techwhirl.com/building-an-nda-compliant-portfolio/)
- Remote Staff: tell the former client or employer how you intend to showcase the work; don't assume a yes; some allow redacted versions. — [Remote Staff](https://www.remotestaff.ph/?p=17353)
- Millo: "Just ask for permission anyway". A reader comment advises asking "via recordable communication (email, text, google voicemail data)". Another reader said her former employer "allowed me to use almost everything I wanted" (anecdote). — [Millo](https://millo.co/can-i-include-my-previous-in-house-work-in-my-portfolio)
- Freelancers Union sample clause (pre-2018): it lists the purposes (competitions, design publications, education, marketing the designer's business) and gives the client credit. — [Freelancers Union](https://blog.freelancersunion.org/2014/06/06/what-if-client-forbids-you-including-something-your-portfolio/)
- terms.law hypothetical scenarios (**search summary only**): "Negotiate portfolio rights BEFORE starting work, not after". — [terms.law](https://terms.law/NDA/scenarios/contractor/case-studies.html)
- ProZ forum (opinion): a former employer never answered emails, LinkedIn messages or contact forms. The poster asked whether linking to already-published work without reproducing it would be acceptable; the thread gives no authoritative answer. — [ProZ](https://dut.proz.com/forum/getting_established/364063-advice_on_portfolio_permissions.html)
- Upwork Community (opinion): agreement can come "either before or after you work on the project". — [Upwork Community](https://community.upwork.com/t5/Freelancers/Client-Permission-for-Portfolio-Items/td-p/1179124)

### Inferences
This checklist is my synthesis of the sources, tailored to the asker's items.
1. **Who and why:** your name, role and dates of engagement; the portfolio URL; the purposes (job search **and** freelance services). Say plainly that the site is commercial.
2. **Itemised yes/no list:**
   - (a) Naming DGSI as employer.
   - (b) Naming each LGU client and app.
   - (c) App icons.
   - (d) Official store screenshots (re-hosted).
   - (e) Re-created screens with fictional data, with or without LGU brand colours.
   - (f) LGU seals or logos.
   - (g) A high-level architecture diagram (attach the draft).
   - (h) Each metric, saying whether it is public.
   - (i) Links to store listings.
   - (j) The private responder app (text description only?).
   - (k) The R&D push-to-talk prototype (description only, or wait until launch?).
   - (l) Role credits and naming colleagues.
   - (m) The technology list.
3. **Safeguards offered:** draft review before publishing; redactions; no code; fictional data only; disclaimers; a credit line ("shown with permission of DGSI"); removal within a set number of days on request.
4. **Scope:** whether permission lasts after you leave; how it can be revoked; whether the LGU must also consent and who asks it.
5. **Form:** a reply by email or a signed letter from an authorised officer (HR, legal or management). Keep the record. Do not treat silence as consent; the "silence = consent" idea comes only from an unofficial 2017 Upwork forum claim.
6. **Expected responses:** approval subject to review or redaction; "after launch only"; partial approval (names and links yes, screenshots or architecture no); or refusal. If refused, use the description-only and clean-room patterns from Q2.

## Q6. Risk realism: how do such disputes usually play out?

### Takeaway
Reported disputes over portfolio display usually end informally: the owner asks for removal and the person complies. Escalation takes the form of cease-and-desist letters, takedown notices to the hosting platform, and for current employees, discipline. I found no reported lawsuit over ordinary portfolio display. The well-publicised firings and legal actions involve unreleased products or leaked source code. In the Philippines, dismissal over a disclosure needs substantial evidence of a wilful breach and a proportionate penalty.

### Cited Findings
- Millo (opinion): "If you use client work in your portfolio and get a request to remove it, the solution is simple: comply." If a company did sue, "the company would first send a cease and desist letter". — [Millo](https://millo.co/can-i-include-my-previous-in-house-work-in-my-portfolio)
- Freelancers Union (pre-2018, opinion): posting work the client has forbidden could expose you to a lawsuit. — [Freelancers Union](https://blog.freelancersunion.org/2014/06/06/what-if-client-forbids-you-including-something-your-portfolio/)
- TechWhirl (pre-2018): a former employer could "blackball" you. — [TechWhirl](https://techwhirl.com/building-an-nda-compliant-portfolio/)
- NN/g (2023): "Future employers perusing your portfolio may view your sloppy handling of sensitive data as a negative signal." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/maintain-ux-portfolio/)
- Apple, October 2017 (**search summary only**): an Apple engineer lost his job after his daughter posted a hands-on video of the then-unreleased iPhone X, filmed on Apple's campus and showing codenames and staff QR codes. Apple asked for the video to be removed. Apple did not confirm the firing; the daughter did. — [AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/17/10/28/apple-sacks-iphone-x-engineer-after-daughter-posts-hands-on-video-to-youtube)
- Twitter, March 2023 (**search summary only**): Twitter filed a DMCA notice and GitHub disabled a repository holding leaked Twitter source code. Twitter also asked a federal court in Northern California to make GitHub identify the uploader; reports said a former employee was suspected. — [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2023/03/27/twitter-hunts-suspected-former-employee-leaked-source-code-github/); [Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2023/03/26/business/twitter-says-parts-its-source-code-were-leaked-online)
- Philippines: in *Interadent v. Simbillo* (G.R. No. 207315, 2016; fetched), the employer dismissed the employee over a Facebook post it called a disclosure of confidential information. The Labor Arbiter and NLRC upheld the dismissal; the CA and Supreme Court reversed it: "No company information or corporate record was divulged"; "A lighter penalty would have sufficed". — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2016/nov2016/gr_207315_2016.html)
- Other Philippine disclosure-dismissal cases (**search summary only**; final outcomes not verified):
  - *Yonzon v. Coca-Cola Bottlers Philippines* (2021): disclosure of co-workers' salaries in an NLRC pleading.
  - *Corpuz v. Citigroup* (2024): dismissal for unauthorised disclosure of a customer's account information; the Labor Arbiter found it valid.
  
  — [chanrobles](https://chanrobles.com/cralaw/2024junedecisions.php?id=189); [ndvlaw commentary](https://ndvlaw.com/can-online-company-criticism-justify-employee-dismissal/)
- Respicio (2025): using or disclosing a former employer's trade secret is actionable (injunction and damages) even without a non-compete. — [Respicio & Co.](https://www.respicio.ph/commentaries/ownership-of-employee-developed-work-formats-after-resignation)

### Inferences
- **Current employee:** the realistic risk is internal discipline for breaking policy, from a warning up to dismissal in serious cases. Philippine tribunals review whether real confidential information was disclosed, whether the breach was wilful, and whether the penalty was proportionate (Interadent). Showing non-public items (the responder app, the R&D prototype, security architecture) raises the risk far more than linking to public store listings.
- **Former employee:** the realistic risk is a removal request or cease-and-desist letter, or a takedown notice to the host. Damages claims are possible for trade-secret disclosure or copyright infringement, but I found no reported case over ordinary portfolio display.
- **Reputation:** future employers read how you handle confidential work as a trust signal (NN/g).
- **Most exposed items in the current portfolio:**
  - Re-hosted official screenshots and icons (direct copying of others' copyrighted and trademarked assets).
  - Anything about the private responder app and the unreleased push-to-talk prototype.
  - Detailed architecture of public-safety systems.
  - Exact LGU brand replicas.
  - Internal metrics.

### Gaps
- I found no reported Philippine dispute about portfolio display and no frequency data on how often removal requests escalate.
