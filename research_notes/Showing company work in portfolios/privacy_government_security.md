# Privacy, public-sector and security limits on showing LGU public-safety app work in a personal portfolio (Philippines, law as of 23 Oct 2026)

Verification legend:
- **Verified** means the primary text was opened in this session. Some privacy.gov.ph pages blocked the fetch tool (HTTP 403), so they were downloaded directly and read locally.
- **Snippet only** means the source appeared only in a search-engine summary and the page itself was not opened.
- **Not verified** means no primary source could be opened.

Scope: this note covers privacy, government-information and security limits only. Copyright, trademark, employment-contract and app-store-policy questions belong to other researchers and are touched only where they intersect.

## 1. Data Privacy Act (RA 10173), 2016 IRR and NPC issuances: do screenshots with real personal data violate it, and does fictional or synthetic data avoid it?

### Takeaway
Screenshots showing real users' or staff's names, phone numbers, locations, incident reports, ID images, photos or call logs are "processing" of personal information, often sensitive personal information. A self-promotional portfolio has no evident lawful basis for that processing. A DGSI employee who posted them would match the actor named in the unauthorized-disclosure offence: Sec. 32 applies to a controller's or processor's "officials, employees or agents". That employee is also bound by a statutory confidentiality duty that survives the end of employment (Sec. 20(e)).

Data that identifies no real person is outside the Act, whether it is wholly fictional or irreversibly anonymised. That follows from the statutory definition and is consistent with NPC opinions. No NPC opinion specifically on portfolios, demos or sample/test data was found.

### Cited Findings
**Definitions (verified)**
- **Personal information:** "any information whether recorded in a material form or not, from which the identity of an individual is apparent or can be reasonably and directly ascertained by the entity holding the information, or when put together with other information would directly and certainly identify an individual." Source: RA 10173 Sec. 3(g), [RA 10173 (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html). The same text is quoted in NPC AO 2020-043, fn. 3.
- **Processing:** "any operation or any set of operations performed upon personal information including, but not limited to, the collection, recording, organization, storage, updating or modification, retrieval, consultation, use, consolidation, blocking, erasure or destruction of data." Source: Sec. 3(j), [RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html).
- **Consent:** "any freely given, specific, informed indication of will, whereby the data subject agrees to the collection and processing of personal information about and/or relating to him or her. Consent shall be evidenced by written, electronic or recorded means." Source: Sec. 3(b), [RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html).
- **Sensitive personal information** includes:
  - race, ethnic origin, marital status, age, colour, and religious, philosophical or political affiliations;
  - health, education, genetic or sexual life, or "any proceeding for any offense committed or alleged to have been committed by such person";
  - information "issued by government agencies peculiar to an individual which includes, but not limited to, social security numbers, previous or current health records, licenses or its denials, suspension or revocation, and tax returns";
  - information classified by EO or statute.

  Source: Sec. 3(l)(1)-(4), [RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html). The same list is reproduced in [MC 15 s. 2023](https://lawphil.net/executive/mc/mc2023/mc_15_2023.html).
- **Personal information controller (PIC):** "a person or organization who controls the collection, holding, processing or use of personal information ... The term excludes: (1) A person or organization who performs such functions as instructed by another person or organization; and (2) An individual who collects, holds, processes or uses personal information in connection with the individual's personal, family or household affairs." Source: Sec. 3(h), [RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html).
- The IRR adds: "There is control if the natural or juridical person or any other body decides on what information is collected, or the purpose or extent of its processing." Source: IRR Sec. 3(m), [2016 IRR (privacy.gov.ph)](https://privacy.gov.ph/implementing-rules-regulations-data-privacy-act-2012/).

**Scope exclusions (verified)**
- Sec. 4 opens: "This Act does not apply to the following". The exclusions include:
  - (a) information about government officers or employees relating to their position or functions;
  - (b) "Information about an individual who is or was performing service under contract for a government institution", insofar as it relates to the services performed;
  - (d) "Personal information processed for journalistic, artistic, literary or research purposes".

  Source: [RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html).
- The IRR narrows these exclusions:
  - They apply "only to the minimum extent of collection, access, use, disclosure or other processing necessary to the purpose, function, or activity concerned".
  - The officer and contractor items cover only "Information processed for purpose of allowing public access to information that fall within matters of public concern". For contractors this includes "the name of the individual and the terms of his or her contract".
  - The media item reads: "Personal information processed for journalistic, artistic or literary purpose, in order to uphold freedom of speech, of expression, or of the press".
  - Research must be "intended for a public benefit".
  - Controllers and processors "remain subject to the requirements of implementing security measures for personal data protection".

  Source: IRR Sec. 5, [IRR](https://privacy.gov.ph/implementing-rules-regulations-data-privacy-act-2012/).

**Principles and lawful basis (verified)**
- Processing must adhere to "the principles of transparency, legitimate purpose and proportionality". Data must be "Collected for specified and legitimate purposes determined and declared before, or as soon as reasonably practicable after collection", and later processed only in ways compatible with those purposes. Source: Sec. 11 and 11(a), [RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html).
- Sec. 12 lists the lawful criteria:
  - consent;
  - a contract with the data subject;
  - a legal obligation;
  - the vital interests of the data subject;
  - national emergency, public order and safety, or public authority functions;
  - legitimate interests not overridden by the data subject's fundamental rights.
- Sec. 13: "The processing of sensitive personal information and privileged information shall be prohibited", except in the cases it lists. Source: [RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html).

**Processor and staff duties (verified)**
- Sec. 14: a PIC that subcontracts must ensure "proper safeguards are in place to ensure the confidentiality of the personal information processed, prevent its use for unauthorized purposes". In addition, "The personal information processor shall comply with all the requirements of this Act". Source: [RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html).
- IRR Sec. 44(b): the outsourcing contract must require the processor to:
  - "(1) Process the personal data only upon the documented instructions of the personal information controller";
  - "(2) Ensure that an obligation of confidentiality is imposed on persons authorized to process the personal data";
  - "(7) At the choice of the personal information controller, delete or return all personal data to the personal information controller after the end of the provision of services".

  IRR Sec. 43 requires safeguards to "prevent its use for unauthorized purposes". Source: [IRR](https://privacy.gov.ph/implementing-rules-regulations-data-privacy-act-2012/).
- Sec. 20(e): "The employees, agents or representatives of a personal information controller who are involved in the processing of personal information shall operate and hold personal information under strict confidentiality if the personal information are not intended for public disclosure. This obligation shall continue even after leaving the public service, transfer to another position or upon termination of employment or contractual relations." Source: [RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html).

**Offences and penalties (verified)**
- **Sec. 25, unauthorized processing:** applies to "persons who process personal information without the consent of the data subject, or without being authorized under this Act or any existing law".
  - Personal information: 1-3 years and PHP 500,000-2,000,000.
  - Sensitive personal information: 3-6 years and PHP 500,000-4,000,000.
- **Sec. 28, processing for unauthorized purposes:** applies to "persons processing personal information for purposes not authorized by the data subject, or otherwise authorized under this Act or under existing laws".
  - Personal information: 1 year 6 months to 5 years and PHP 500,000-1,000,000.
  - Sensitive personal information: 2-7 years and PHP 500,000-2,000,000.
- **Sec. 31, malicious disclosure:** applies to any PIC or PIP "or any of its officials, employees or agents, who, with malice or in bad faith, discloses unwarranted or false information".
- **Sec. 32, unauthorized disclosure:** "(a) Any personal information controller or personal information processor or any of its officials, employees or agents, who discloses to a third party personal information not covered by the immediately preceding section without the consent of the data subject".
  - Personal information: 1-3 years and PHP 500,000-1,000,000.
  - Sensitive personal information, under (b): 3-5 years and PHP 500,000-2,000,000.
- **Sec. 33:** a combination or series of the acts in Secs. 25-32 carries 3-6 years and PHP 1,000,000-5,000,000.
- **Sec. 34:** where the offender is a juridical person, "the penalty shall be imposed upon the responsible officers".
- **Sec. 35:** "The maximum penalty in the scale of penalties respectively provided for the preceding offenses shall be imposed when the personal information of at least one hundred (100) persons is harmed, affected or involved".

Source for all of the above: [RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html).

**NPC opinions and guidance (verified unless marked)**
- **NPC Advisory Opinion No. 2020-043 (4 Nov 2020), "Disclosure of screenshots of a private conversation"** ([NPC AO 2020-043](https://www.privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Redacted-Advisory-Opinion-No.-2020-043.pdf)):
  - Sending out a screenshot "will only come under the scope of the DPA if personal data is indeed involved – if the conversation/screenshot itself allows for the identification of the parties. If it is simply the content of the conversation, with names and other identifiers redacted or cropped out of the screenshot, it might not be within the scope of the DPA."
  - On the household exclusion: "depending on the attendant circumstances, the taking of the screenshot and its transmittal to a third party, may not fall under the abovementioned exclusion".
  - Disclosure "without consent of the parties involved, or without some other lawful basis for processing of personal data under the DPA, may be construed as unauthorized processing".
- **NPC Advisory Opinion No. 2020-046 (9 Nov 2020):** posting class rosters that include education data on a school's official social media is processing of sensitive personal information. It needs a Sec. 13 basis, either consent or a regulation. For names alone, the NPC said: "Names are classified as personal information, the processing of which may be anchored on the legitimate interest of the school, subject to the three-part test on purpose, necessity, and balance." Source: [NPC AO 2020-046](https://www.privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Redacted-Advisory-Opinion-No.-2020-046.pdf).
- **NPC press release on its online-learning bulletin (16 Feb 2021):** "A strict social media policy entails prohibiting teachers and other school personnel from using personal data collected in an official capacity and/or during an official school activity for personal purposes (e.g. posting in their social media accounts)." It also warns of "the possible data privacy consequences of posting screen captures, images, videos, chats". Source: [NPC press release](https://privacy.gov.ph/privacy-commissions-updated-online-learning-guidelines-advise-schools-to-enforce-social-media-policy/).
- **NPC Advisory Opinion No. 2017-027 (23 Jun 2017)** ([NPC AO 2017-027](https://privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/NPC_AdvisoryOpinionNo._2017-027.pdf)):
  - "anonymized data, in its truest sense, is not considered as personal information and thus, falls outside of the ambit of the DPA".
  - Quoting the EU Article 29 Working Party: "removing directly identifying elements in itself is not enough to ensure that identification of the data subject is no longer possible".
  - "If the company has access to the complete records, then the fact that the processing results in anonymized data, would not exempt the company from the coverage of the data privacy act".
- **NPC Advisory Opinion No. 2019-036 (3 Jul 2019):** "aggregate or statistical data are not considered as personal information under the DPA since such data cannot identify an individual". Adding case numbers brings the data back into scope. Source: [NPC AO 2019-036](https://privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/v1Redacted-Advisory-Opinion-No.-2019-036.pdf).
- **NPC Circular 2023-06 (Security of Personal Data in the Government and the Private Sector):** "The Circular took effect on March 30, 2024", and "Circular 2023-06 repeals Circular 16-01". Source: [NPC FAQ on Circular 2023-06](https://privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/v12-19-2024_FAQ-NPC-Circular-2023-06_NNJ_JDN.pdf). Secondary summaries say it covers both sectors and requires personnel training, acceptable-use policies and access controls ([Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/change/9243-national-privacy-commission-npc-circular-2023-06-on-security-of-personal-data-in-the-government-and-private-sector), snippet only).

**What the apps actually process (verified)**

It also states "The app was launched last March 27, 2025", with the Municipal Mayor attending. Source: [Taytay LGU post](https://www.taytayrizal.gov.ph/single-post/smile-safe-taytay-app).
- The Google Play listing's data-safety summary reads "Location, Personal info and 2 others". The description refers to sending "location for immediate tracking and response". Source: [Google Play listing](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.dgsi.sagiptaytay&hl=en).

### Inferences
- **Real-data screenshots are personal information.** A screenshot of a live LGU app or an admin/responder console showing real residents' names, mobile numbers, addresses, GPS pins, incident narratives, selfies or ID images is personal information (Sec. 3(g)). ID images, medical emergencies and alleged offences are likely sensitive personal information (Sec. 3(l)).
- **No lawful basis fits.** Re-using that data for self-promotion is a purpose the data subjects never consented to and the LGU never declared (Sec. 11(a)). None of the Sec. 12 grounds clearly fits. For sensitive data, Sec. 13's default prohibition applies, and none of its exceptions covers a portfolio.
- **Publishing makes the developer a controller.** Deciding to publish means deciding the purpose of processing, which is "control" under IRR Sec. 3(m). Neither PIC exclusion fits:
  - the developer is not acting on the LGU's or DGSI's instructions;
  - a public portfolio with a commercial side is not "personal, family or household affairs" (compare AO 2020-043's caution).
- **Criminal exposure is personal.** Secs. 25 and 28 attach to "persons", and Sec. 32 to a controller's or processor's "employees or agents". If 100 or more people appear, as in a dashboard or list view, Sec. 35 requires the maximum penalty.
- **Responders and LGU staff.** The IRR's officer exclusion covers only position-related facts (the fact of employment, title, office address and telephone, classification, salary range, name on documents), and only "for purpose of allowing public access" to matters of public concern. Live locations, personal mobile numbers and photos are outside that list, so treat them as personal data.
- **The media and research exemptions are unlikely to help.** A self-promotional portfolio is unlikely to count as "journalistic, artistic or literary" processing or public-benefit "research". Even if it did, the IRR limits the exemption to the minimum extent necessary. No NPC opinion addresses portfolios, so this is analysis, not a ruling.
- **Fictional or synthetic data is outside the DPA.** Wholly invented names, numbers and addresses that correspond to no real person are not "personal information" (Sec. 3(g); AO 2017-027; AO 2020-043). Three cautions follow from the NPC's anonymisation reasoning:
  - Do not "anonymise" a real capture by blurring a name. The map pin, timestamp or incident type can still single someone out.
  - Do not create "synthetic" records by lightly editing production records. If the developer still has the real records, AO 2017-027 says the result is not exempt.
  - Do not use real colleagues' names, numbers or faces without their consent.
- **Aggregate metrics are not personal data** (AO 2019-036), such as total users or total reports. They may still be confidential client information (see section 2).

### Gaps
- No NPC advisory opinion, circular or decision was found on developer portfolios, product demos, sample/test data, or re-using production screenshots. This is a gap, not a permission.
- It is not confirmed, app by app, that the LGU is the controller and DGSI the processor. That depends on the contracts or MOAs and the apps' privacy notices, which were not reviewed.
- The operative text of NPC Circular 2023-06 was not opened, only the NPC FAQ. For example, any specific rule on test environments or personnel confidentiality undertakings is unverified.
- NPC Circular 2023-04 (consent guidelines) was not reviewed.

## 2. Government clients: may a private developer say the employer built apps for named LGUs? What is already public? Do Philippine government ICT contracts carry confidentiality clauses (RA 12009 and its IRR, or RA 9184)?

### Takeaway
No Philippine statute found forbids truthfully stating that a company or person built an app for a named LGU. For the Taytay app, the relationship is already public: the Play listing names Dynamic Global Soft Inc. as developer, and the LGU publicises the app on its website.

The binding limits come from contracts, not from statute. RA 12009 is transparency-oriented and contains no general publicity or confidentiality duty on contractors. Agency terms of reference, however, sometimes contain sweeping confidentiality and "owner publishes" clauses; MARINA's 2026 TOR is an example. The government's own FOI exceptions (public safety, law enforcement, privacy, proprietary information) are a good guide to what a contractor should not publish.

### Inferences
- **Naming the client is low-risk in public law where the facts are already public.** That applies when the LGU publicises the app and the store listing shows Dynamic Global Soft Inc. as developer. The remaining risk is contractual (DGSI-LGU and DGSI-employee) and depends on documents not seen.
- **Contracts can be far stricter than the statute.** A MARINA-style clause ("barred from disclosing all gathered information"; "exclusive right to publish"), read literally, would cover screenshots, metrics, architecture and outputs. Until DGSI's LGU contracts are checked, a developer working through DGSI should assume similar terms may exist.
- **The government's FOI exceptions are a useful proxy for what a contractor should not publish.** These include public-safety and law-enforcement detail (dispatch procedures, responder workflows), personal data, and proprietary information. This applies most strongly to the privately distributed responder app and the R&D push-to-talk prototype.
- **Procurement data is public; DGSI's pricing and approach may not be.** Contract values or terms posted on PhilGEPS are public facts, but DGSI's own pricing and technical approach may still be proprietary.
- **Metrics:** public numbers are the safe source, such as store install bands or figures the LGU itself announced. Internal incident or user statistics are not personal data in aggregate (AO 2019-036), but they may be official or confidential information.

- The other LGUs' listings and posts (San Jose del Monte, Isabela City, Abra de Ilog) and Eventbook were not checked.
- No GPPB-standard confidentiality clause in the NGPA PBDs' general conditions was verified, because the GPPB PDF returned 404.

## 3. Security: rules restricting disclosure of the architecture of government or emergency-response systems, and what security standards say

### Takeaway
No Philippine statute or executive issuance found generally prohibits a private person from describing, at a high level, how an LGU citizen app is built:
- MC 78 s. 1964 (classified matter) binds government offices. It reaches contractors only through department rules or contract terms, and only for matter actually classified.
- EO 58 s. 2024 (National Cybersecurity Plan 2023-2028) imposes nothing on private persons.
- No critical information infrastructure (CII) protection law or EO was found.
- RA 10175 punishes attacks and making credentials available, not descriptions.

Security standards still treat exposure of system details as a weakness (CWE-200 and CWE-497) and as reconnaissance input (MITRE ATT&CK T1593). They also say secrecy must not be the security control (NIST "Open Design"). The practical line: generic diagrams are fine; operational specifics are not.

### Cited Findings
**MC 78 s. 1964 (verified)**
- **Date and scope:** dated 14 Aug 1964. It is to be "enforced and observed in all departments, bureaus, offices and agencies of the government", including provincial, municipal and city offices. It protects "Official matter which requires protection in the interest of national security".
- **Four classification levels:**
  - TOP SECRET, e.g. "Critical information about cryptography".
  - SECRET.
  - CONFIDENTIAL: "Any officer is authorized to assign CONFIDENTIAL classification to any matter in the performance of his duties".
  - RESTRICTED, e.g. "Departmental books of instruction and training and technical documents intended for official use only".
- **Dissemination:** limited to "properly cleared persons whose official duties require knowledge or possession thereof". No person "shall convey orally, visually or by written communication any classified matter outside his own Department unless such disclosure has been processed and cleared by the Department Head".
- **Contractors:** department heads must issue supplementary rules on "security arrangements in dealing with government contractors engaged in projects concerning classified matter" (para. 55b).
- **Sanctions:** violations are "dealt with administratively", "without prejudice to any criminal prosecution".

Source: [MC 78 (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/executive/mc/mc1964/mc_78_1964.html). MC 78 was amended by MC 196 s. 1968, which added communications security ([lawphil](https://lawphil.net/executive/mc/mc1968/mc_196_1968.html), snippet only).
- **EO 608 s. 2007** (30 Mar 2007) created a security clearance system for government personnel with access to "classified matters as defined under Memorandum Circular No. 78 as amended by Memorandum Circular No. 196". It provides: "Any unauthorized disclosure, sharing, publication or use of the information contained in the classified documents or materials shall be considered a grave offense and shall be punishable in accordance with civil service rules and regulations". Source: [EO 608 (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/executive/execord/eo2007/eo_608_2007.html). A search summary claimed EO 608 says MC 78 is "not deemed repealed pending further review"; that sentence was not in the lawphil text.

**EO 58 s. 2024 / NCSP 2023-2028 and CII**
- EO 58 was signed 4 Apr 2024 (verified). National agencies and GOCCs "are hereby directed" to adopt cybersecurity plans consistent with the NCSP, while "all LGUs are hereby encouraged, to formulate, adopt and implement their cybersecurity plans and strategies". The order does not mention CII. It imposes no obligations on private entities or contractors and has no provision on withholding information about government systems. Source: [EO 58 (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/executive/execord/eo2024/eo_58_2024.html).
- A US-ASEAN Business Council summary says CII is not within the NCSP's scope, and that DICT intends a CII-protection EO pending a CIIP law. Source: [US-ASEAN](https://www.usasean.org/article/philippines-president-issues-executive-order-strengthen-cybersecurity), snippet only.
- Searches found CIIP bills, e.g. Senate Bill 1382 (19th Congress), but no enacted CIIP law or CIIP EO. Snippet only; bills are not law.

**RA 10175, Cybercrime Prevention Act (verified)**
- Sec. 4(a)(1), illegal access: "The access to the whole or any part of a computer system without right".
- Sec. 4(a)(5), misuse of devices: "The use, production, sale, procurement, importation, distribution, or otherwise making available, without right, of: ... (bb) A computer password, access code, or similar data by which the whole or any part of a computer system is capable of being accessed with intent that it be used for the purpose of committing any of the offenses under this Act".
- Sec. 3(j), critical infrastructure: computer systems or networks "so vital to this country that the incapacity or destruction of or interference with such system and assets would have a debilitating impact on security, national or economic security, national public health and safety".
- Sec. 8: Sec. 4(a) offences "committed against critical infrastructure" carry "reclusion temporal or a fine of at least Five hundred thousand pesos (PhP500,000.00)".

Source: [RA 10175 (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10175_2012.html).

**Security standards (verified unless marked)**
- **NIST SP 800-123**, *Guide to General Server Security* (July 2008), Sec. 2.4 Server Security Principles: "Open Design--System security should not depend on the secrecy of the implementation or its components." Source: [NIST SP 800-123](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-123.pdf).
- **OWASP Developer Guide:** open design "is in contrast to security by obscurity where the security of the software is dependent upon the obscuring of the design itself. When software is architected using the open design concept, the review of the design itself will not result in the compromise of the safeguards in the software." Source: [OWASP Developer Guide](https://devguide.owasp.org/en/02-foundations/03-security-principles/).
- **CWE-200 and CWE-497:** "CWE-200: Exposure of Sensitive Information to an Unauthorized Actor" and "CWE-497 Exposure of Sensitive System Information to an Unauthorized Control Sphere" are listed among the CWEs mapped to OWASP Top 10:2021 A01. Source: [OWASP Top 10:2021 A01](https://top10.owasp.org/2021/A01_2021-Broken_Access_Control/index.html). cwe.mitre.org was unreachable from this session, so the CWE entries' own descriptions are not verified.
- **MITRE ATT&CK T1593, "Search Open Websites/Domains":** "Adversaries may search freely available websites and/or domains for information about victims that can be used during targeting. Information about victims may be available in various online sites, such as social media, new sites [sic], or those hosting information about business operations such as hiring or requested/rewarded contracts." Source: [MITRE ATT&CK T1593](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1593/).
- **OWASP WSTG-INFO-10, "Map Application Architecture":** the test objective is to "Understand the architecture of the application and the technologies in use" by building "a clear picture of how the application works, and which technologies and components are in place". Source: [OWASP WSTG source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OWASP/wstg/master/document/4-Web_Application_Security_Testing/01-Information_Gathering/10-Map_Application_Architecture.md).

### Inferences
- **MC 78 does not classify LGU citizen-app architecture by default.** An authorised officer must classify it. If DGSI ever received LGU material marked RESTRICTED or CONFIDENTIAL (network diagrams, SOPs), publishing it would breach that regime as applied through contract, and possibly other laws. Ask DGSI whether any project material carried markings.
- **RA 10175's "critical infrastructure" concept affects attackers, not describers.** An LGU emergency-response system plausibly fits the definition ("national public health and safety"), but that changes penalties for attackers, not the legality of describing the system. The real RA 10175 risk for a portfolio is accidentally publishing credentials, API keys, tokens or default passwords in screenshots or diagrams, although Sec. 4(a)(5) also requires intent.
- **High level is fine; attack-surface specifics are not.** A generic "mobile app → API → database → push/maps services" diagram is ordinary and low-risk. CWE-200/497 and ATT&CK reconnaissance concern details that map the attack surface:
  - hostnames, IPs, ports, cloud project IDs, storage bucket names, API routes;
  - authentication and role logic, admin or dispatcher console URLs;
  - integration endpoints, third-party vendors with versions;
  - push-to-talk server topology;
  - known weaknesses.
- **"Who built each part" diagrams name individuals.** That feeds the same reconnaissance; ATT&CK lists sites about "hiring or requested/rewarded contracts" as a source. Listing only one's own contributions and generic team roles reduces this.
- **"Open Design" does not license a contractor to publish details.** It means the system should not rely on secrecy; that is the owner's design goal. The decision to accept disclosure risk belongs to the system owner (the LGU) and DGSI.

### Gaps
- No DICT circular specifically governing disclosure of LGU system information was found. DICT issuances (e.g. on CII or government cloud) were not opened. Not verified.
- The status of CIIP bills in the 20th Congress (2025-2026) was not checked against the Senate or House trackers.
- The implications of RA 4200 (Anti-Wiretapping Act) for publishing real push-to-talk or call audio were not researched.

## 4. Using LGU names, seals or branding in a way that could imply government endorsement

### Takeaway
Truthfully naming an LGU client is generally fine. The risk lies in displaying LGU seals or logos, or in wording that suggests official partnership, endorsement or agency. The relevant rules:
- IP Code Sec. 169.1 gives a civil action for false or misleading representations about affiliation, sponsorship or approval.
- Some LGUs have seal ordinances with penal clauses.
- At the extreme, RPC Art. 177 punishes pretending to be a public officer.
- RA 8491's prohibitions centre on the national flag; no express bar on commercial use of LGU seals was found in its text.

### Cited Findings
- **RA 8293 Sec. 169.1:** liable to a civil action for damages and injunction is any person who, in connection with goods or services, "uses in commerce any word, term, name, symbol, or device, or any combination thereof, or any false designation of origin, false or misleading description of fact, or false or misleading representation of fact, which: (a) Is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive as to the affiliation, connection, or association of such person with another person, or as to the origin, sponsorship, or approval of his or her goods, services, or commercial activities by another person". Source: [RA 8293 (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html).
- **RA 7160 (Local Government Code) Sec. 22:** under (a)(3), every LGU has the power "To have and use a corporate seal". Under (b), new or changed seals "shall be registered with the Department of the Interior and Local Government". Source: [RA 7160 (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1991/ra_7160_1991.html).
- **RA 8491 (Flag and Heraldic Code):**
  - Sec. 44: "Any government entity, including the military, may adopt appropriate coat-of-arms, administrative seals, logo, insignia, badges, patches, and banners ... as may be authorized by Congress or the Office of the President".
  - Sec. 45 requires filing these with the Institute.
  - Sec. 34(c)(6) prohibits using the national flag "As trademarks, or for industrial, commercial or agricultural labels or designs".
  - Sec. 50 sets a fine of P5,000-20,000 and/or imprisonment of up to 1 year for violations of the Act.

  Source: [RA 8491 (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1998/ra_8491_1998.html). No express prohibition on commercial use of LGU seals was found in the text reviewed. A Respicio & Co. commentary asserts broader restrictions ([Respicio](https://www.respicio.ph/commentaries/legal-requirements-for-using-government-logos-and-seals-in-the-philippines)); it is secondary, and its claims were not traced to statutory text.
- **Example local rule:** Batangas City Ordinance No. 22-06 s. 2024, "An ordinance prescribing and regulating the use of the Batangas City official seal, and imposing penalties for unofficial or unauthorized use thereof, and for other purposes". Source: [Batangas City](https://www.batangascity.gov.ph/web/about-the-city/resolutions-and-ordinances/ordinances/ordinances-2024/7215-ordinance-no-22-06-s-2024). Search results show similar ordinances elsewhere, e.g. Tabaco City Ord. 015-2017 and Lingayen Ord. 79 s. 2019 (snippet only).
- **RPC Art. 177** (text as shown on lawphil): "Any person who, under pretense of official position, shall perform any act pertaining to any person in authority or public officer, without being lawfully entitled to do so, shall suffer the penalty of prision correccional ...". Source: [RPC (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/acts/act1930/act_3815_1930.html).
- Taytay's website navigation includes a "Municipal Seal" page, so the seal is a formally adopted symbol. Source: [Taytay LGU site](https://www.taytayrizal.gov.ph/single-post/smile-safe-taytay-app).

### Inferences
- **The Services page brings Sec. 169.1 into play.** It makes the portfolio commercial ("in commerce", "commercial activities"). Phrases such as "Official developer of the Municipality of Taytay", "LGU partner" or "Trusted by four LGUs" beside seals, or seals in a client-logo strip, could suggest affiliation or approval by the LGU. They could also suggest that the individual, rather than DGSI, holds the client relationship.
- **Safer wording:** plain attributed text, for example "Mobile developer at Dynamic Global Soft Inc.; contributed to the Smile SAFE Taytay citizen app (client: Municipality of Taytay, Rizal)". Use no seals, and add a short disclaimer such as "Client names identify past work; no endorsement implied."
- **App icons and brand colours** are the app's branding, not the LGU seal. They carry lower endorsement risk, but IP and permission questions remain (outside this note).
- **Check local ordinances first.** Before using any LGU mark, check whether Taytay, San Jose del Monte, Isabela City and Abra de Ilog have seal-use ordinances. None was checked.

### Gaps
- No Philippine rule specifically governing how private contractors advertise government clients was found; contrast US GSAR 552.203-71 in section 5.
- The four LGUs' seal ordinances were not checked.
- RA 10951's adjustments to RPC fine amounts were not checked.

## 5. Comparators: GDPR treatment of screenshots containing personal data, and public-sector contractor confidentiality practice

### Takeaway
GDPR reaches the same results:
- identifiable people in a screenshot means personal data;
- truly anonymous data is out of scope;
- the household exemption is lost once there is any professional or commercial link;
- people working under a processor may process data only on the controller's instructions.

Mature public-sector contracting regimes (US DFARS and GSAR) write in default bans on releasing contract information and on implying government endorsement. That mirrors what Philippine agencies can and sometimes do put in a TOR, as in the MARINA example.

### Cited Findings
- **GDPR Recital 26:** "The principles of data protection should apply to any information concerning an identified or identifiable natural person." ... "The principles of data protection should therefore not apply to anonymous information" ... "personal data rendered anonymous in such a manner that the data subject is not or no longer identifiable." Pseudonymised data remains personal data. Source: [GDPR Recital 26](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-26/).
- **GDPR Recital 18:** "This Regulation does not apply to the processing of personal data by a natural person in the course of a purely personal or household activity and thus with no connection to a professional or commercial activity." Source: [GDPR Recital 18](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-18/).
- **GDPR Art. 29:** "The processor and any person acting under the authority of the controller or of the processor, who has access to personal data, shall not process those data except on instructions from the controller, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law." Source: [GDPR Art. 29](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-29-gdpr/).
- The NPC itself relies on EU sources (Article 29 WP Opinion 05/2014 and GDPR Recital 26) for its anonymisation standard. Sources: [NPC AO 2019-036](https://privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/v1Redacted-Advisory-Opinion-No.-2019-036.pdf); [NPC AO 2017-027](https://privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/NPC_AdvisoryOpinionNo._2017-027.pdf).
- **US DFARS 252.204-7000, Disclosure of Information (OCT 2016):** "The Contractor shall not release to anyone outside the Contractor's organization any unclassified information, regardless of medium (e.g., film, tape, document), pertaining to any part of this contract or any program related to this contract", unless:
  - the Contracting Officer has given prior written approval;
  - the information "is otherwise in the public domain before the date of release"; or
  - a fundamental-research exception applies.

  Approval requests must identify the specific information and be submitted at least 10 business days in advance, and the clause flows down to subcontracts. Source: [DFARS 252.204-7000](https://www.acquisition.gov/dfars/252.204-7000-disclosure-information.).
- **US GSAR 552.203-71, Restriction on Advertising (SEP 1999):** "The Contractor shall not refer to this contract in commercial advertising or similar promotions in such a manner as to state or imply that the product or service provided is endorsed or preferred by the White House, the Executive Office of the President, or any other element of the Federal Government, or is considered by these entities to be superior to other products or services." Source: [GSAR 552.203-71](https://www.acquisition.gov/gsam/552.203-71).
- **Philippine example:** the MARINA TOR confidentiality and "exclusive right to publish" clause (section 2). Source: [MARINA](https://marina.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Part-I_NGPA_PBDs_Consulting_MARINA-MIP.pdf).

### Inferences
- **The household exemption does not cover a commercial portfolio.** The CJEU's Lindqvist judgment (C-101/01, 2003) is commonly cited for the point that internet publication to an indefinite audience is not "household" activity. It is not verified here because EUR-Lex returned an empty page. Recital 18's "no connection to a professional or commercial activity" independently supports the same result for a portfolio with a Services page.
- **The DFARS "public domain" carve-out is a reasonable benchmark.** For a Philippine portfolio, facts the LGU or store listing has already published are low-risk, and anything non-public needs approval.

### Gaps
- The text of the Lindqvist judgment was not verified.
- No EU or UK regulator guidance specifically on developer portfolios was searched.

## 6. How the limits shift by role: employee (regular duties or not), contractor/commissioned, current vs former, with vs without NDA

### Takeaway
Privacy duties do not depend on having signed an NDA. DPA Sec. 20(e) confidentiality and the Sec. 25/28/32 offences reach any employee, agent or contractor who handled LGU app data, and Sec. 20(e) survives resignation or termination. Under IRR Sec. 44(b)(2), DGSI must ensure that its staff who process personal data are under a confidentiality obligation, so some undertaking or policy likely exists even without a separate NDA.

An NDA, the LGU contract and company policy add contractual limits on non-personal information: client names, architecture, metrics, screenshots. Without an NDA, those limits shrink to the statutory floor (DPA, the RPC secrets articles, classified-matter rules if any) plus whatever DGSI's LGU contract requires DGSI to impose on its staff.

### Cited Findings
- Sec. 20(e) confidentiality "shall continue even after leaving the public service, transfer to another position or upon termination of employment or contractual relations". Source: [RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html).
- Secs. 31-32 apply to a PIC's or PIP's "officials, employees or agents"; Secs. 25 and 28 apply to "persons" processing without authority or for unauthorized purposes. Source: [RA 10173](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10173_2012.html).
- IRR Sec. 44(b)(2): the processor must "Ensure that an obligation of confidentiality is imposed on persons authorized to process the personal data". IRR Sec. 44(b)(4): the processor must "Not engage another processor without prior instruction from the personal information controller", with the same obligations flowing down. Source: [IRR](https://privacy.gov.ph/implementing-rules-regulations-data-privacy-act-2012/).
- **RPC Art. 291:** "any manager, employee, or servant who, in such capacity, shall learn the secrets of his principal or master and shall reveal such secrets" is punished with arresto mayor and, in the original text, a fine not exceeding 500 pesos. **RPC Art. 292** covers the "person in charge, employee or workman of any manufacturing or industrial establishment who, to the prejudice of the owner thereof, shall reveal the secrets of the industry of the latter". Source: [RPC (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/acts/act1930/act_3815_1930.html).
- RA 12009 Sec. 37: in direct procurement for science, technology and innovation, IP in commissioned goods belongs to the procuring entity unless otherwise agreed. Source: [RA 12009](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2024/ra_12009_2024.html).
- GDPR Art. 29 is the EU analogue: persons acting under a processor's authority process data only on the controller's instructions. Source: [GDPR Art. 29](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-29-gdpr/).

### Inferences
| Situation | Privacy (DPA) | Government / contract | Security |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current employee, LGU work done as regular duties | Full Sec. 20(e) duty; Secs. 25/28/32 exposure for any real data shown | Bound through DGSI's LGU contract and company policy; get DGSI's written approval (DGSI may itself need LGU consent if its contract has a MARINA-type clause) | Follow DGSI/LGU security rules; any classification markings apply |
| Employee, work outside regular duties (own projects) | DPA relevant only if real people's data appears | No client-side limits from LGUs or DGSI; employment-contract IP and moonlighting terms are outside this note | Normal care |
| Former employee | Sec. 20(e) duty continues; same offences | Post-employment confidentiality depends on contract wording; without it, RPC Art. 291 is the main residual rule (its reach to ex-employees not researched); public facts remain usable | Same |
| Contractor or commissioned work directly for an LGU | Likely an "agent" or processor; IRR Sec. 44 terms bind directly | Directly bound by TOR/contract confidentiality and IP clauses; RA 12009 Sec. 37 if STI direct procurement | Same |
| Sub-contractor to DGSI | Bound through IRR Sec. 44(b)(4) flow-down | Bound by the sub-contract | Same |
| With NDA | Statutory duties unchanged | NDA governs non-personal information and may bar naming the client or showing UI; look for publicity or portfolio carve-outs | Same |
| Without NDA | Statutory duties unchanged | Fewer contractual limits, but implied duties and company policies may exist (outside scope); still safest to seek consent | Same |

- In every role, real personal data from LGU systems should never appear. Non-public material needs written consent from DGSI, and ideally from the LGU. That covers the responder app, the R&D push-to-talk prototype, internal metrics and detailed architecture.

### Gaps
- The actual employment contract, NDA, company policies and LGU contract terms are unknown, so they could not be analysed.
- Labor Code and Civil Code doctrine on post-employment confidentiality and NDA enforceability is outside this note (other researchers).
- RPC Art. 291's application to former employees and to client secrets (as opposed to the employer's own) was not researched.

## 7. Practical safe patterns (synthesis)

### Takeaway
Show your role, the problem, your decisions and the stack, not the client's data or operational security details. In practice:
- put fictional data in rebuilt screens;
- link to the official store listings;
- name clients factually, without seals;
- keep architecture diagrams generic;
- use only public metrics;
- get written sign-off from DGSI (and, through DGSI, the LGU) for anything beyond what is already public.

### Cited Findings
- Identifiability is the DPA trigger. Cropped or redacted screenshots "might not be within the scope of the DPA" ([NPC AO 2020-043](https://www.privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Redacted-Advisory-Opinion-No.-2020-043.pdf)), but "removing directly identifying elements in itself is not enough" ([NPC AO 2017-027](https://privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/NPC_AdvisoryOpinionNo._2017-027.pdf)).
- Aggregate figures are not personal data. Source: [NPC AO 2019-036](https://privacy.gov.ph/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/v1Redacted-Advisory-Opinion-No.-2019-036.pdf).
- The NPC expects organisations to bar personnel from using personal data "collected in an official capacity ... for personal purposes (e.g. posting in their social media accounts)". Source: [NPC 2021](https://privacy.gov.ph/privacy-commissions-updated-online-learning-guidelines-advise-schools-to-enforce-social-media-policy/).
- Public-domain information is the standard carve-out in contractor disclosure clauses. Source: [DFARS 252.204-7000](https://www.acquisition.gov/dfars/252.204-7000-disclosure-information.).
- Misleading claims of affiliation or approval are actionable. Source: [RA 8293 Sec. 169.1](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html).
- Security should not depend on secrecy, but attackers use public websites for reconnaissance. Sources: [NIST SP 800-123](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-123.pdf); [MITRE ATT&CK T1593](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1593/).

### Inferences (recommended patterns; analysis, not legal advice)
1. **Screens.** Build replicas from scratch, not by editing real captures, and fill them with obviously fictional data:
   - names such as "Juan Dela Cruz (sample)";
   - 0917-000-0000-style numbers;
   - map pins in neutral spots or on a generic basemap;
   - no real IDs, faces or plate numbers; use licensed stock or generated avatars and "SAMPLE" ID cards.

   Label them "Recreated UI with fictional data". Never show admin or dispatcher views with real incident queues. Strip EXIF and other metadata from images.
2. **Official store screenshots.** Link to the Google Play and App Store listings rather than re-hosting them. If re-hosting (an IP question handled elsewhere), first confirm they show only demo data.
3. **Client naming.** Use factual, attributed text ("at Dynamic Global Soft Inc., for the Municipality of Taytay"). Do not use seals or LGU logos. Avoid "official", "partner" and "endorsed". Add a no-endorsement line, and use the app's public name with its store link.
4. **Non-public apps (responder app, push-to-talk prototype).** Describe your role, the problem and the stack in prose. Show screens only as synthetic replicas approved by DGSI. Give no protocol, server or deployment details.
5. **Architecture diagrams.**
   - Keep them generic and logical: client app, API layer, database, push and maps services, admin web.
   - Omit hostnames, IPs, ports, cloud project IDs, storage bucket or queue names, API paths, library versions, authentication internals, security controls and known gaps.
   - Show no detailed personal-data flows, and mark diagrams "simplified".
   - For "who built what", list only your own contributions.
   - Have DGSI's security lead or data protection officer review them before publishing.
6. **Metrics.** Use only public numbers (store install bands, or LGU-announced figures with a link), or claims about your own work (e.g. "reduced report-submission steps from X to Y"). Avoid incident counts and response-time statistics unless the LGU has published them.
7. **Secrets check.** Scan every image, video and diagram for keys, tokens, URLs and environment names before publishing. This is the RA 10175 Sec. 4(a)(5) risk area and the CWE-200/497 concern.
8. **Permissions file.** Keep DGSI's (and, if obtained, the LGU's) written approvals, and remove anything on request.
9. **Personal projects** owned outright can be shown in full detail, provided they contain no other people's personal data.

### Gaps
- These patterns are inferences from the cited rules. No Philippine regulator or court guidance on developer portfolios was found.
