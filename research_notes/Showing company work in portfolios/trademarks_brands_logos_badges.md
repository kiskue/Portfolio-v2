# Trademarks, brands, logos, LGU seals and app-store badges on a developer's portfolio (Philippines primary; US/EU and platform rules as comparators)

Research date: 2026-10-23. Statutes were read in full from lawphil.net (RA 8293, RA 10372, RA 8491, RA 7160, RA 10175, Civil Code). EU texts came from the EU Publications Office's official EUR-Lex documents, US cases from the Ninth Circuit's opinion archive and public.resource.org, and platform and brand rules from the live official pages, plus one archived official Google page. Quotes are exact unless marked "(via fetch tool)", which means a page summariser returned the quote and I could not re-check it character by character. RA 10372 (approved 28 Feb 2013) is the latest enacted amendment to RA 8293 that I found. It amended Secs. 6, 7, 9A, 171.3, 171.9, 171.12-13, 180, 181, 183, 184.1, 185.1, 188.1, 191, 198, 203, 204.1, 208, 212, 216, 218.1, 220A, 226 and 230, and did **not** touch the trademark part (Secs. 121-170) or Secs. 171.11, 176 and 178 ([RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html)). Copyright ownership, confidentiality/NDA and privacy appear here only where they overlap with marks. Other researchers cover them.

## Overall question: when is naming or showing someone else's marks on a portfolio lawful referential use, and when does it risk infringement, unfair competition, false endorsement or a platform breach?

### Takeaway
Philippine law has no express "nominative fair use" clause. Even so, every Philippine claim a portfolio could trigger turns on likely confusion or deception about source, affiliation, sponsorship or approval: infringement (Secs. 147/155), unfair competition (Sec. 168, which also needs deception or fraud), and false designation or false association (Sec. 169). Accurate, text-only identification of an employer, a client, an LGU and an app is therefore low-risk. Logos, app icons, LGU seals, store badges, brand-coloured look-alikes and "Clients / Trusted by" framing raise the risk, because they borrow the owner's visual identity and can signal endorsement. Two further layers can forbid uses that trademark law might tolerate:
- platform and brand licences: Apple's badge licence covers only the developer-account holder's own apps, and several tech brands restrict logo use;
- LGU seal ordinances: Batangas City, for example, needs a Mayor's letter of authority before its seal appears on any website, and bars commercial use.

Contracts (NDA, publicity and IP-assignment clauses) bind on top of all of this.

### Cited Findings
- Rights in a registered mark reach only confusing uses in trade: "The owner of a registered mark shall have the exclusive right to prevent all third parties not having the owner's consent from using in the course of trade identical or similar signs or containers for goods or services which are identical or similar to those in respect of which the trademark is registered where such use would result in a likelihood of confusion. In case of the use of an identical sign for identical goods or services, a likelihood of confusion shall be presumed." — [RA 8293 Sec. 147.1](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Infringement means using a registered mark or a copy of it "in commerce ... in connection with the sale, offering for sale, distribution, advertising of any goods or services ... on or in connection with which such use is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive". — [RA 8293 Sec. 155.1](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Unfair competition needs deception: "Deception, passing off and fraud upon the public are still the key elements that must be present for unfair competition to exist." — [Coca-Cola Bottlers Phils., Inc. v. Gomez, G.R. No. 154491, Nov. 14, 2008](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2008/nov2008/gr_154491_2008.html)
- False association creates civil liability for using "any word, term, name, symbol, or device" in commerce that "Is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive as to the affiliation, connection, or association of such person with another person, or as to the origin, sponsorship, or approval of his or her goods, services, or commercial activities by another person". — [RA 8293 Sec. 169.1(a)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- The trademark part's only fair-use style limit covers descriptive use: third parties using their own names, addresses, pseudonyms, geographical names or exact indications, "Provided, That such use is confined to the purposes of mere identification or information and cannot mislead the public as to the source of the goods or services." — [RA 8293 Sec. 148](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- US nominative fair use applies where "First, the product or service in question must be one not readily identifiable without use of the trademark; second, only so much of the mark or marks may be used as is reasonably necessary to identify the product or service; and third, the user must do nothing that would, in conjunction with the mark, suggest sponsorship or endorsement by the trademark holder." — [New Kids on the Block v. News America Publishing, Inc., 971 F.2d 302, 308 (9th Cir. 1992)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/971/971.F2d.302.90-56258.90-56219.html)
- In the US, a broker's use of the stylized Lexus mark and "Lexus L" logo "was more use of the mark than necessary and suggested sponsorship or endorsement by Toyota. This is true: The Tabaris could adequately communicate their message without using the visual trappings of the Lexus brand." — [Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc. v. Tabari, 610 F.3d 1171 (9th Cir. 2010), slip op. No. 07-55344](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf)
- An EU trade mark does not let its owner stop use of "the EU trade mark for the purpose of identifying or referring to goods or services as those of the proprietor of that trade mark", but "Paragraph 1 shall only apply where the use made by the third party is in accordance with honest practices in industrial or commercial matters." — [Regulation (EU) 2017/1001 Art. 14(1)(c), 14(2)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/1001/oj)
- In the EU, referential use is lawful "unless the mark is used in a way that may create the impression that there is a commercial connection between the other undertaking and the trade mark proprietor, and in particular that the reseller's business is affiliated to the trade mark proprietor's distribution network or that there is a special relationship between the two undertakings." — [BMW v Deenik, C-63/97, judgment of 23 Feb 1999](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:61997CJ0063)
- Platform licence layer: Apple licenses the App Store badge "only in connection with Your applications that are available for download on the App Store ("Applications") and only while You are a member of the Apple Developer Program." — [Apple App Store Marketing Guidelines, App Store Marketing Artwork License Agreement §1](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/marketing/guidelines/)
- LGU ordinance layer, by example: it is unlawful "To affix, depict, display, or post the Batangas City Official Seal, or replica thereof, on any document, thing, item, or material, in or within any internet website, or social media platform, except when authorized by an LOA from the City Mayor". — [Batangas City Ordinance No. 22-06 s. 2024, Sec. 5(f)](https://www.batangascity.gov.ph/web/images/Ordinances2024/ORD20242206.pdf)
- Contract layer: "Obligations arising from contracts have the force of law between the contracting parties and should be complied with in good faith." — [Civil Code Art. 1159](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1949/ra_386_1949.html). Parties "may establish such stipulations, clauses, terms and conditions as they may deem convenient, provided they are not contrary to law, morals, good customs, public order, or public policy." — [Civil Code Art. 1306](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1949/ra_386_1949.html)
- General Civil Code backstop: "Unfair competition in agricultural, commercial or industrial enterprises or in labor through the use of force, intimidation, deceit, machination or any other unjust, oppressive or highhanded method shall give rise to a right of action by the person who thereby suffers damage." — [Civil Code Art. 28](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1949/ra_386_1949.html)
- Criminal exposure exists in principle. Sec. 170 imposes "a criminal penalty of imprisonment from two (2) years to five (5) years and a fine ranging from Fifty thousand pesos (P50,000) to Two hundred thousand pesos(P200,000)" for acts under Secs. 155, 168 and 169.1 — [RA 8293 Sec. 170](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html). Crimes under special laws "committed by, through and with the use of information and communications technologies" carry a penalty "one (1) degree higher" — [RA 10175 Sec. 6](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2012/ra_10175_2012.html)

**How the analysis shifts by relationship (inference, building on Secs. 178.3-178.4, Civil Code Arts. 1159/1306 and Secs. 168-169):**
- *Employee, work within regular duties.* Copyright in code, screens and art goes to the employer ([Sec. 178.3(b)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)) unless agreed otherwise. Marks never belong to the employee: they belong to the registrant ([Sec. 122](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)) or, for unregistered goodwill, to the business ([Sec. 168.1](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)). Truthful text references are the default safe zone. Logos, icons, screenshots and badges need the owner's consent (DGSI, and the LGU for LGU-branded material).
- *Employee, work outside regular duties.* Copyright belongs to the employee "even if the employee uses the time, facilities and materials of the employer" ([Sec. 178.3(a)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)). If the work carries DGSI or LGU branding, the mark rules above still apply.
- *Contractor / commissioned work.* The commissioning party owns "the work", but "the copyright thereto shall remain with the creator, unless there is a written stipulation to the contrary" ([Sec. 178.4](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)). That says nothing about the client's marks, which stay the client's. Contracts often contain IP-assignment and portfolio or publicity clauses, and those control (Arts. 1159/1306).
- *Current employee.* The extra risk is implying that DGSI offers, backs or routes the freelance services (Sec. 168.3(b); Sec. 169.1(a)). Avoid DGSI logos near the Services page. Company brand policies may also apply.
- *Former employee.* Past-tense, truthful statements of history are the clearest referential use. The US court in *Welles* applied nominative fair use to "a former playmate's use of the Playboy mark to describe herself and her website" ([Tabari, n.1](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf)). Leaving the company does not create a licence to logos or badges.
- *With an NDA or publicity clause.* A contract can forbid even references that trademark law allows, such as naming the client at all ([Arts. 1159/1306](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1949/ra_386_1949.html)). Read the clause before anything else.
- *Without an NDA.* The default trademark, unfair-competition and ordinance rules above apply. Trade-secret and confidentiality duties may still exist (covered elsewhere).

**Safe wording and notice patterns (inference):**
- Project card: "Smile SAFE Taytay: citizen public-safety app that DGSI (my employer, 20XX-20XX) builds for the Municipality of Taytay, Rizal. My role: [specific modules]. Store listing: View on Google Play · Available on the App Store (published by [publisher exactly as shown on the listing])."
- Site-wide notice, modelled on the *Tabari* disclaimer, which the court called "relevant" though "not required": "Company, LGU, app and technology names are trademarks or official marks of their respective owners. They appear only to identify work I did while employed at DGSI and do not imply endorsement by, or affiliation with, DGSI, any LGU, Google or Apple. Google Play and the Google Play logo are trademarks of Google LLC. App Store is a trademark of Apple Inc., registered in the U.S. and other countries."
- Realistic exposure: the likeliest consequence of over-broad use is a takedown or demand letter, an employer or contract dispute, or a platform brand complaint, not prosecution. Criminal liability under Sec. 170 needs the same confusion or deception elements, and truthful, labelled references sit far from that line.

### Gaps
- I found no Philippine Supreme Court decision and no IPOPHL issuance that squarely addresses nominative or referential use of another's mark (résumés, portfolios, client lists). The Philippine analysis rests on the statutory elements plus persuasive foreign authority.
- Unknown facts that change the answer: the contract terms (NDA, IP assignment, publicity or non-use-of-name clauses); current or former status; whether DGSI's name and logo and the LGU app names and icons are registered at IPOPHL (not searched); who holds the developer account on each store listing (DGSI or the LGU).

## 1. RA 8293 trademark provisions (exact current text). Is "I built the Smile SAFE Taytay app for the Municipality of Taytay while at DGSI" lawful? When does showing a client's logo or app icon imply sponsorship or affiliation?

### Takeaway
The exact texts tie every relevant right to use in trade that is likely to confuse or deceive (Secs. 147, 155), to deceptive passing off (Sec. 168), or to confusion about affiliation, sponsorship or approval (Sec. 169). A truthful, specific, text-only statement such as "At DGSI I built the Smile SAFE Taytay app, which DGSI delivered for the Municipality of Taytay" is very likely lawful under these provisions. The wording must not overstate the relationship: it should not suggest the LGU hired the developer personally, overstate a team contribution, or suggest DGSI or the LGU endorse the freelance services. Logos and app icons push toward implied sponsorship. **Correction to the brief:** insignia of the Philippines and its political subdivisions are in **Sec. 123.1(b)**, not 123.1(c). Sec. 123.1(c) covers names, portraits and signatures of living individuals. Both are bars to *registration*, not restrictions on third-party *use*.

### Cited Findings
- Sec. 121.1: "'Mark' means any visible sign capable of distinguishing the goods (trademark) or services (service mark) of an enterprise and shall include a stamped or marked container of goods". Sec. 121.3: "'Trade name' means the name or designation identifying or distinguishing an enterprise". — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Sec. 122: "The rights in a mark shall be acquired through registration made validly in accordance with the provisions of this law." — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Sec. 123.1: "A mark cannot be registered if it: (a) Consists of immoral, deceptive or scandalous matter, or matter which may disparage or falsely suggest a connection with persons, living or dead, institutions, beliefs, or national symbols, or bring them into contempt or disrepute; (b) Consists of the flag or coat of arms or other insignia of the Philippines or any of its political subdivisions, or of any foreign nation, or any simulation thereof; (c) Consists of a name, portrait or signature identifying a particular living individual except by his written consent, or the name, signature, or portrait of a deceased President of the Philippines, during the life of his widow, if any, except by written consent of the widow;" — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Sec. 147.1 is quoted in the Overall section. Sec. 147.2 extends well-known registered marks to dissimilar goods only "Provided, That use of that mark in relation to those goods or services would indicate a connection between those goods or services and the owner of the registered mark: Provided further, That the interests of the owner of the registered mark are likely to be damaged by such use." — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Sec. 148 (full text): "Use of Indications by Third Parties for Purposes Other than those for which the Mark is Used. - Registration of the mark shall not confer on the registered owner the right to preclude third parties from using bona fide their names, addresses, pseudonyms, a geographical name, or exact indications concerning the kind, quality, quantity, destination, value, place of origin, or time of production or of supply, of their goods or services: Provided, That such use is confined to the purposes of mere identification or information and cannot mislead the public as to the source of the goods or services." — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Sec. 155: "Any person who shall, without the consent of the owner of the registered mark: 155.1. Use in commerce any reproduction, counterfeit, copy, or colorable imitation of a registered mark or the same container or a dominant feature thereof in connection with the sale, offering for sale, distribution, advertising of any goods or services including other preparatory steps necessary to carry out the sale of any goods or services on or in connection with which such use is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive; or 155.2. Reproduce, counterfeit, copy or colorably imitate a registered mark or a dominant feature thereof and apply such reproduction, counterfeit, copy or colorable imitation to labels, signs, prints, packages, wrappers, receptacles or advertisements intended to be used in commerce upon or in connection with the sale, offering for sale, distribution, or advertising of goods or services on or in connection with which such use is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive, shall be liable in a civil action for infringement by the registrant for the remedies hereinafter set forth: Provided, That the infringement takes place at the moment any of the acts stated in Subsection 155.1 or this subsection are committed regardless of whether there is actual sale of goods or services using the infringing material." — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Sec. 158 (damages need knowledge): the owner "shall not be entitled to recover profits or damages unless the acts have been committed with knowledge that such imitation is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive. Such knowledge is presumed if the registrant gives notice that his mark is registered by displaying with the mark the words '"Registered Mark" or the letter R within a circle or if the defendant had otherwise actual notice of the registration." — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Sec. 165.2: "(a) Notwithstanding any laws or regulations providing for any obligation to register trade names, such names shall be protected, even prior to or without registration, against any unlawful act committed by third parties. (b) In particular, any subsequent use of the trade name by a third party, whether as a trade name or a mark or collective mark, or any such use of a similar trade name or mark, likely to mislead the public, shall be deemed unlawful." — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Sec. 168.1: "A person who has identified in the mind of the public the goods he manufactures or deals in, his business or services from those of others, whether or not a registered mark is employed, has a property right in the goodwill of the said goods, business or services so identified, which will be protected in the same manner as other property rights." Sec. 168.2: "Any person who shall employ deception or any other means contrary to good faith by which he shall pass off the goods manufactured by him or in which he deals, or his business, or services for those of the one having established such goodwill, or who shall commit any acts calculated to produce said result, shall be guilty of unfair competition, and shall be subject to an action therefor." — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Sec. 168.3: "(a) Any person, who is selling his goods and gives them the general appearance of goods of another manufacturer or dealer ... which would be likely to influence purchasers to believe that the goods offered are those of a manufacturer or dealer, other than the actual manufacturer or dealer ...; (b) Any person who by any artifice, or device, or who employs any other means calculated to induce the false belief that such person is offering the services of another who has identified such services in the mind of the public; or (c) Any person who shall make any false statement in the course of trade or who shall commit any other act contrary to good faith of a nature calculated to discredit the goods, business or services of another." — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Sec. 169.1 (full text): "Any person who, on or in connection with any goods or services, or any container for goods, uses in commerce any word, term, name, symbol, or device, or any combination thereof, or any false designation of origin, false or misleading description of fact, or false or misleading representation of fact, which: (a) Is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive as to the affiliation, connection, or association of such person with another person, or as to the origin, sponsorship, or approval of his or her goods, services, or commercial activities by another person; or (b) In commercial advertising or promotion, misrepresents the nature, characteristics, qualities, or geographic origin of his or her or another person's goods, services, or commercial activities, shall be liable to a civil action for damages and injunction provided in Sections 156 and 157 of this Act by any person who believes that he or she is or is likely to be damaged by such act." — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Confusion of business, i.e. a false belief in a connection, is actionable: "the defendant's product is such as might reasonably be assumed to originate with the plaintiff, and the public would then be deceived either into that belief or into the belief that there is some connection between the plaintiff and defendant which, in fact, does not exist." Also: "confusion of business exists when the products are non-competing but related enough to produce confusion of affiliation." — [McDonald's Corp. v. L.C. Big Mak Burger, Inc., G.R. No. 143993, Aug. 18, 2004](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2004/aug2004/gr_143993_2004.html)
- Sec. 169.1 tracks the US Lanham Act §43(a)(1) almost word for word, including "affiliation, connection, or association" and "origin, sponsorship, or approval". — compare [RA 8293 Sec. 169.1](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html) with [15 U.S.C. §1125(a)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1125)

### Inferences
- **The sample sentence.** "I built the Smile SAFE Taytay app for the Municipality of Taytay while at DGSI" uses the app name, the LGU name and the employer name as words to *identify* things, not as the developer's own source identifier. Under Secs. 147/155 there is then little room for "likelihood of confusion" as to source. Under Sec. 168 there is no passing off. Under Sec. 169.1(a) the question is whether readers would infer that DGSI or the LGU sponsor or approve the developer's own services. Three refinements lower that risk:
  - make clear the contracting party was DGSI ("DGSI's project for the Municipality of Taytay");
  - describe the developer's actual role instead of "I built", if a team built it (Sec. 169.1(b) misrepresentation risk);
  - use past tense once employment ends.
- **Is a portfolio "use in commerce"?** A site with a Services page that solicits paid work is likely commercial "advertising of ... services" (Sec. 155.1) or "commercial advertising or promotion" (Sec. 169.1(b)). A pure résumé page is weaker on that element. Treat the portfolio as commercial.
- **DGSI is in the same line of business** (software services) as the developer's freelance offer. If DGSI's name or logo is a registered mark, Sec. 147.1 presumes confusion for "an identical sign for identical goods or services". The better reading is that a résumé reference is not use of the sign *for* the developer's services, but this is unsettled in Philippine law, and it makes DGSI's *logo* the most sensitive of the employer marks. Sec. 168.3(b) ("offering the services of another") is the specific unfair-competition risk if the employer's branding sits next to "hire me" calls to action.
- **When a logo or icon implies sponsorship.** These factors come from *Tabari*'s "visual trappings", *New Kids* n.7 (no "distinctive lettering"), *Deenik*'s "special relationship", *Big Mak*'s "confusion of affiliation", and the Batangas ordinance's finding that people seeing the seal "are likely to believe" the displayer is official or approved:
  - logos used where words would do;
  - placement in a hero or header, or on the Services page;
  - headings like "Clients", "Partners" or "Trusted by";
  - testimonials;
  - present-tense language;
  - official colours used as the site theme;
  - any government seal;
  - no disclaimer.
- **Unregistered marks.** Registration matters for Secs. 147/155 (Zuneca: ownership is acquired through registration). Secs. 168 and 169 and the trade-name rule in Sec. 165 apply "whether or not a registered mark is employed" or "without registration". So the LGU app names are protected against confusing use even if unregistered.

### Gaps
- Whether "Smile SAFE Taytay", the other LGU app names, "Eventbook" or "DGSI" are registered at IPOPHL was not checked (IPOPHL eTMView search not run).
- No Philippine decision found that construes "use in commerce" for personal websites or portfolios.

## 2. Philippine cases and IPOPHL guidance on descriptive, referential/nominative use, comparative advertising, or using another's mark in one's own promotional material

### Takeaway
I found no Philippine case or IPOPHL guidance on nominative or referential use. The closest Supreme Court authorities supply the elements:
- what trademarks do (*Mirpuri*);
- confusion of business as a false belief in a connection (*Big Mak*);
- unfair competition requires deception and passing off, not mere unfairness (*Coca-Cola v. Gomez*; *Shang*);
- using a geographic name to identify a location is not unfair competition (*Shang*);
- ownership comes from registration (*Zuneca*).

Philippine courts routinely draw on US authority in trademark cases, so US nominative-use reasoning is plausible persuasive authority, though not binding.

### Cited Findings
- Functions of a trademark: "to point out distinctly the origin or ownership of the goods to which it is affixed; to secure to him, who has been instrumental in bringing into the market a superior article of merchandise, the fruit of his industry and skill; to assure the public that they are procuring the genuine article; to prevent fraud and imposition; and to protect the manufacturer against substitution and sale of an inferior and different article as his product." — [Mirpuri v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 114508, Nov. 19, 1999](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1999/nov1999/gr_114508_1999.html)
- Elements of unfair competition: "The essential elements of an action for unfair competition are (1) confusing similarity in the general appearance of the goods, and (2) intent to deceive the public and defraud a competitor." — [McDonald's Corp. v. L.C. Big Mak Burger, G.R. No. 143993, Aug. 18, 2004](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2004/aug2004/gr_143993_2004.html)
- Scope of Sec. 168: "The law does not thereby cover every unfair act committed in the course of business; it covers only acts characterized by 'deception or any other means contrary to good faith' in the passing off of goods and services as those of another who has established goodwill in relation with these goods or services, or any other act calculated to produce the same result." And: "One of the essential requisites in an action to restrain unfair competition is proof of fraud; the intent to deceive must be shown before the right to recover can exist." — [Coca-Cola Bottlers Phils., Inc. v. Gomez, G.R. No. 154491, Nov. 14, 2008](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2008/nov2008/gr_154491_2008.html)
- Geographic names. Quoting *Great Southern Bank*: "[d]escriptive geographical terms are in the 'public domain' in the sense that every seller should have the right to inform customers of the geographical origin of his goods." The Court held "there can be no unfair competition without this element [fraud]" and that the use "was meant only to identify, or at least associate, their real estate project/s with its geographical location." — [Shang Properties Realty Corp. v. St. Francis Development Corp., G.R. No. 190706, July 21, 2014](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2014/jul2014/gr_190706_2014.html)
- Ownership via registration: "the language of the IP Code provisions clearly conveys the rule that ownership of a mark is acquired through registration; (ii) the intention of the lawmakers was to abandon the rule that ownership of a mark is acquired through use". — [Zuneca Pharmaceutical v. Natrapharm, Inc., G.R. No. 211850, Sept. 8, 2020 (En Banc)](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2020/sep2020/gr_211850_2020.html)
- Philippine courts rely on US sources in trademark cases. *Shang* quotes *Great Southern Bank v. First Southern Bank* and *Burke-Parsons-Bowlby Corp. v. Appalachian Log Homes* ([Shang](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2014/jul2014/gr_190706_2014.html)). *Big Mak* adopts Callman's two types of confusion via *Sterling Products* ([Big Mak](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2004/aug2004/gr_143993_2004.html)).
- Comparative advertising, comparators only. The EU lists as a potentially prohibited use "using the sign in comparative advertising in a manner that is contrary to Directive 2006/114/EC" ([Reg. 2017/1001 Art. 9(3)(f)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/1001/oj)). *New Kids* said trademark protection "does not extend to rendering newspaper articles, conversations, polls and comparative advertising impossible" ([New Kids](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/971/971.F2d.302.90-56258.90-56219.html)).
- Status of reform, **not law**. Search results (not opened in full; the IPOPHL page blocked automated retrieval) describe IP Code amendment bills pending in the 20th Congress: non-traditional marks, geographical indications, site blocking. I found no enacted amendment after RA 10372. — [IPOPHL news (not verified)](https://www.ipophil.gov.ph/news/ipophl-congress-push-key-reforms-to-ip-code-amid-rapid-tech-economic-change/); [BusinessMirror, Aug. 19, 2025 (not verified)](https://businessmirror.com.ph/2025/08/19/ipophl-ip-code-update-eyed-ai-off-the-table/); [RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html)

### Inferences
- Because unfair competition requires deception or fraud (*Coca-Cola*; *Shang*), a portfolio that accurately credits DGSI and the LGU, and disclaims affiliation, is hard to characterise as Sec. 168 unfair competition. The live theories are Sec. 169.1(a) (implied affiliation or sponsorship) and, for registered marks, Sec. 155.
- *Shang* supports naming an LGU (a place name) in text to identify where or for whom the work was done. It does not reach seals or logos.

### Gaps
- No Philippine authority on comparative advertising or promotional use of others' marks was reviewed: the Ad Standards Council Code and DTI/Consumer Act rules were not checked.
- No IPOPHL guidance or FAQ on using others' marks was found. The IPOPHL news page could not be retrieved automatically.
- No Philippine decision found on résumés, portfolios or client lists.

## 3. Government names and symbols: RA 8491, RA 7160, local ordinances. Text naming vs seal, logo or LGU-branded app icon

### Takeaway
National law regulates how government entities **adopt and register** their seals, not private display:
- RA 8491 lets "any government entity" adopt coats-of-arms, seals and logos, filed with the National Historical Institute (now NHCP). Its express bans on trademark, merchandise and advertising use cover only the **national flag**.
- RA 7160 gives each LGU a corporate seal registered with DILG.

The real restriction on showing an LGU seal comes from **local ordinances**. Batangas City's 2024 ordinance bans displaying its seal, or a replica, on any website without a Mayor's letter of authority (LOA). LOAs are only for local, non-commercial, non-political purposes, and fines go up to ₱1,000/₱2,000/₱3,000. I did not find whether Taytay, San Jose del Monte, Isabela City or Abra de Ilog have similar ordinances.

Naming an LGU in text is materially different: I found no restriction, geographic names are public domain (*Shang*), and LGU contracts must be posted publicly. An LGU-branded app icon that incorporates the seal should be treated like the seal.

### Cited Findings
- RA 8491 title: "AN ACT PRESCRIBING THE CODE OF THE NATIONAL FLAG, ANTHEM, MOTTO, COAT-OF-ARMS AND OTHER HERALDIC ITEMS AND DEVICES OF THE PHILIPPINES". Sec. 3(e): "'Symbol' shall mean any conventional sign which reveals man's achievement and heroism (for orders and decorations), identification, authority and a sign of dignity (for coat-of-arms, logo and insignia)". — [RA 8491](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1998/ra_8491_1998.html)
- RA 8491 Sec. 44: "Any government entity, including the military, may adopt appropriate coat-of-arms, administrative seals, logo, insignia, badges, patches, and banners; and initiate awards, citations, orders or decorations; as may be authorized by Congress or the Office of the President." Sec. 45: "Such heraldic devices and items shall be filed with the Institute for recording and evaluation as to precedence, design, customs and traditions. The Institute shall promulgate the corresponding rules and regulations which shall be submitted for approval to the Office of the President or to Congress." — [RA 8491](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1998/ra_8491_1998.html)
- RA 8491's commercial and advertising bans are flag-specific. Sec. 34 prohibits using "the flag ... - Batangas City Ordinance No. 22-06 s. 2024, "AN ORDINANCE PRESCRIBING AND REGULATING THE USE OF THE BATANGAS CITY OFFICIAL SEAL, AND IMPOSING PENALTIES FOR UNOFFICIAL OR UNAUTHORIZED USE THEREOF, AND FOR OTHER PURPOSES". ### Inferences
- **Naming an LGU in text** ("for the Municipality of Taytay, Rizal") is not restricted by RA 8491, RA 7160 or the IP Code provisions reviewed. It is a truthful geographic and institutional reference (*Shang*; Sec. 148). Under RA 7160 Sec. 22(c), the existence of an LGU's contract is meant to be public. That lowers, but does not remove, sensitivity about naming the LGU as DGSI's client: an NDA or the contract itself may still restrict it.
- **Showing the LGU seal or coat of arms** is the riskiest single element. Seal ordinances like Batangas's expressly cover websites and "replica[s]", and they confine permission to non-commercial local purposes, so a commercial portfolio may not even qualify for an LOA. Seals also signal official approval: Sec. 169.1(a) "approval", plus the Batangas recital. If the seal was designed by LGU employees, Sec. 176.1 makes "exploitation of such work for profit" subject to prior approval, and a Services-page portfolio is arguably commercial.
- **LGU logo or app-brand logo, not the seal.** If an LGU-branded app icon embeds the seal, treat it as the seal. If it is a separate app logo, it is the publisher's mark and copyright work: medium risk, best replaced by text plus a store link.
- **Not covered by RA 8491.** RA 8491's penal clause (Sec. 50) applies to violations of "any of the provisions of this Act", and I found no RA 8491 provision forbidding private persons from displaying LGU seals. Do not cite RA 8491 as banning portfolio display of an LGU seal; cite local ordinances instead.
- **Apps built by DGSI for an LGU are not "works of the Government".** Sec. 171.11 requires creation by a government "officer or employee ... as a part of his regularly prescribed official duties". Their copyright follows Secs. 178.3-178.4 and the DGSI-LGU contract.

### Gaps
- No seal-use ordinance was found for Taytay (Rizal), City of San Jose del Monte (Bulacan), City of Isabela (Basilan) or Abra de Ilog (Occidental Mindoro). Their Sangguniang Bayan or Panlungsod records should be checked.
- No NHCP rules under RA 8491 Sec. 45, and no DILG issuance on LGU seal registration or private display, were retrieved.
- An executive order on national-seal use (EO 141, s. 1999) appeared only in a search snippet and was not verified. It would concern national, not LGU, symbols.

## 4. App icons and official store screenshots (trademarks plus copyrighted artwork of the publisher): what that means for re-hosting them on a portfolio

### Takeaway
An app icon is both the publisher's mark (a "visible sign capable of distinguishing" its goods or services) and copyrighted artwork. Official store screenshots are the publisher's copyrighted marketing material, and they usually show the app's marks and, for LGU apps, often the LGU seal. Re-hosting them is reproduction (copyright) plus display of marks. An employee generally owns neither:
- works made in regular duties belong to the employer;
- for commissioned work, copyright stays with the creator (here DGSI) unless a written stipulation says otherwise.

Fair use is uncertain because commercial nature counts against it. Safer options are linking to the store listing, using text names, and using self-made re-creations with fictional data that omit seals and official logos and are labelled as re-creations.

### Cited Findings
- Definition of mark: "any visible sign capable of distinguishing the goods (trademark) or services (service mark) of an enterprise" — [RA 8293 Sec. 121.1](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Employment works: "In the case of work created by an author during and in the course of his employment, the copyright shall belong to: (a) The employee, if the creation of the object of copyright is not a part of his regular duties even if the employee uses the time, facilities and materials of the employer. (b) The employer, if the work is the result of the performance of his regularly-assigned duties, unless there is an agreement, express or implied, to the contrary." — [RA 8293 Sec. 178.3](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Commissioned works: "In the case of a work commissioned by a person other than an employer of the author and who pays for it and the work is made in pursuance of the commission, the person who so commissioned the work shall have ownership of the work, but the copyright thereto shall remain with the creator, unless there is a written stipulation to the contrary" — [RA 8293 Sec. 178.4](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Fair use as amended: "the fair use of a copyrighted work for criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching including limited number of copies for classroom use, scholarship, research, and similar purposes is not an infringement of copyright." — [RA 10372 Sec. 12 amending RA 8293 Sec. 185.1](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html). The factors that remain include "(a) The purpose and character of the use, including whether such use is of a commercial nature or is for non-profit educational purposes; ... (c) The amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole; and (d) The effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work." — [RA 8293 Sec. 185.1](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Apple's rule for screen content in app marketing: "You are responsible for securing the rights to all materials used in screen content within your app, and you should display fictional account information instead of data from a real person." Also: "Your app screen images, Mac, Apple Watch, iPhone, iPad, iPod touch, and Apple TV product images, or photographs thereof cannot be used in any manner that falsely suggests an association with Apple or is likely to reduce, diminish, or damage the goodwill, value, or reputation associated with the App Store, the Mac App Store, iPhone, iPad, iPod touch, Apple Watch, Apple TV, or Apple itself." — [Apple App Store Marketing Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/marketing/guidelines/)
- Logos and visual identity online: "Imagery, logos and other visual markers may be particularly significant in cyberspace, where anyone can convincingly recreate the look and feel of a luxury brand at minimal expense. It's hard to duplicate a Lexus showroom, but it's easy enough to ape the Lexus site." — [Toyota v. Tabari (9th Cir. 2010)](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf)
- Names vs logos: "a soft drink competitor would be entitled to compare its product to Coca-Cola or Coke, but would not be entitled to use Coca-Cola's distinctive lettering." The newspapers passed because "they do not use the New Kids' distinctive logo or anything else that isn't needed to make the announcements intelligible to readers." — [New Kids, 971 F.2d at 308 & n.7](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/971/971.F2d.302.90-56258.90-56219.html)
- Brand-owner practice: "the name may be used, but not the stylized wording or the logo" — [Meta Open Source Trademark Policy](https://opensource.fb.com/legal/trademark/)
- EU honest practices and replicas: "where the third party presents its product as an imitation or replica of the product bearing the trade mark of which it is not the owner, such use of that mark does not comply with honest practices within the meaning of Article 6(1)(c)." — [Gillette Co. v LA-Laboratories, C-228/03, judgment of 17 Mar 2005, para. 45](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62003CJ0228)

### Inferences
- **Icons.** Re-hosting an app icon next to "I built this" is a visual use of the publisher's mark. That is exactly the kind of "visual trappings" use that *Tabari* and *New Kids* treat as more than necessary when words would do, and that Meta's policy carves out of nominative use. Risk is moderate. It is higher for LGU apps whose icons embed seals, which brings in ordinances.
- **Official screenshots.** They belong to the publisher by employment (Sec. 178.3(b)) or by contract. They may contain the LGU seal and real-looking data. A commercial portfolio weakens the fair-use factor (a). A link to the official listing lets the publisher display its own assets and avoids reproducing them.
- **Self-made replicas with fictional data.** They avoid copying screenshot files and real data; Apple's own guidance prefers fictional data. If they reproduce the app name, logo, seal and brand colours, they still carry the marks and may look official. Label them as re-creations of the developer's own work ("UI re-created for illustration; not the official app; data fictional"), and drop seals and official logos. *Gillette*'s "imitation or replica" point is EU law and concerns goods sold as replicas, so it is only an analogy, but it signals that "replica" framing of another's branded product draws scrutiny.
- **By relationship.** Employee in regular duties: no copyright, so permission is needed for icons and screenshots. Contractor: may hold copyright in their own designs absent a written assignment (Sec. 178.4), but still has no right in the client's marks. Former employee: same as an employee as to ownership. NDA or publicity clause: may forbid any display.
- **Personal projects** that the developer owns and publishes are free of these constraints, subject only to the platform badge rules (Section 6).

### Gaps
- Google Play's and Apple's store Terms of Service on copying listing content (icons, screenshots) were not checked.
- Whether the LGU apps' icons or screenshots incorporate LGU seals was not verified.

## 5. US and EU comparators: nominative fair use (New Kids; Tabari), Lanham Act §43(a) false endorsement; EU Reg. 2017/1001 Art. 14(1)(c) and Directive 2015/2436 Art. 14

### Takeaway
Both comparators protect truthful referential use but police implied connection. US nominative fair use (Ninth Circuit) permits using a mark to refer to the owner's product if it is necessary, uses no more of the mark than needed, and suggests no sponsorship. Logos and stylized marks commonly fail the second and third limbs. Lanham Act §43(a)(1)(A) creates false-endorsement liability for confusion about "affiliation, connection, or association" or "sponsorship, or approval". EU Art. 14(1)(c) permits referential use only "in accordance with honest practices", which the CJEU reads as a duty to act fairly: no impression of a commercial connection, no unfair advantage, no denigration, no "imitation or replica" framing.

### Cited Findings
- Rationale: "Such nominative use of a mark--where the only word reasonably available to describe a particular thing is pressed into service--lies outside the strictures of trademark law: Because it does not implicate the source-identification function that is the purpose of trademark, it does not constitute unfair competition; such use is fair because it does not imply sponsorship or endorsement by the trademark holder." The three-part test is quoted in the Overall section. Also: "Where, as here, the use does not imply sponsorship or endorsement, the fact that it is carried on for profit and in competition with the trademark holder's business is beside the point." — [New Kids on the Block v. News America Publishing, 971 F.2d 302 (9th Cir. 1992)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/971/971.F2d.302.90-56258.90-56219.html) (also indexed at [CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/9009089/new-kids-on-the-block-v-news-america-publishing-inc/))
- *Tabari* restates the test: "we ask whether (1) the product was 'readily identifiable' without use of the mark; (2) defendant used more of the mark than necessary; or (3) defendant falsely suggested he was sponsored or endorsed by the trademark holder." — [Toyota Motor Sales v. Tabari, 610 F.3d 1171 (9th Cir. 2010)](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf)
- *Tabari* on remedies: "If the nominative use does not satisfy all the New Kids factors, the district court may order defendants to modify their use of the mark so that all three factors are satisfied; it may not enjoin nominative use of the mark altogether." Also: "A trademark injunction, particularly one involving nominative fair use, can raise serious First Amendment concerns because it can interfere with truthful communication between buyers and sellers in the marketplace." — [Tabari](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf)
- *Tabari* on disclaimers: the site's disclaimer said "We are not an authorized Lexus dealer or affiliated in any way with Lexus. We are an Independent Auto Broker." The court held: "While not required, such a disclaimer is relevant to the nominative fair use analysis." — [Tabari](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf)
- *Tabari* on burden: "On remand, Toyota must bear the burden of establishing that the Tabaris' use of the Lexus mark was not nominative fair use. A finding of nominative fair use is a finding that the plaintiff has failed to show a likelihood of confusion as to sponsorship or endorsement." — [Tabari](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf)
- Self-description: "In Welles, for instance, we applied our nominative fair use analysis to a former playmate's use of the Playboy mark to describe herself and her website." — [Tabari n.1, citing Playboy Enters. v. Welles, 279 F.3d 796, 801 (9th Cir. 2002)](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/07/08/07-55344.pdf)
- Lanham Act §43(a)(1): "Any person who, on or in connection with any goods or services, or any container for goods, uses in commerce any word, term, name, symbol, or device, or any combination thereof, or any false designation of origin, false or misleading description of fact, or false or misleading representation of fact, which— (A) is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive as to the affiliation, connection, or association of such person with another person, or as to the origin, sponsorship, or approval of his or her goods, services, or commercial activities by another person, or (B) in commercial advertising or promotion, misrepresents the nature, characteristics, qualities, or geographic origin of his or her or another person's goods, services, or commercial activities, shall be liable in a civil action by any person who believes that he or she is or is likely to be damaged by such act." — [15 U.S.C. §1125(a)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1125)
- Dilution exclusion naming nominative use: "Any fair use, including a nominative or descriptive fair use, or facilitation of such fair use, of a famous mark by another person other than as a designation of source for the person's own goods or services, including use in connection with— (i) advertising or promotion that permits consumers to compare goods or services; or (ii) identifying and parodying, criticizing, or commenting upon the famous mark owner or the goods or services of the famous mark owner." — [15 U.S.C. §1125(c)(3)(A)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1125)
- EU Regulation Art. 14(1): "An EU trade mark shall not entitle the proprietor to prohibit a third party from using, in the course of trade: (a) the name or address of the third party, where that third party is a natural person; (b) signs or indications which are not distinctive or which concern the kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, value, geographical origin, the time of production of goods or of rendering of the service, or other characteristics of the goods or services; (c) the EU trade mark for the purpose of identifying or referring to goods or services as those of the proprietor of that trade mark, in particular, where the use of that trade mark is necessary to indicate the intended purpose of a product or service, in particular as accessories or spare parts." Art. 14(2): "Paragraph 1 shall only apply where the use made by the third party is in accordance with honest practices in industrial or commercial matters." Art. 9(3)(e) lists "using the sign on business papers and in advertising" among uses that may be prohibited. — [Regulation (EU) 2017/1001](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/1001/oj) (text retrieved via the Publications Office, CELEX 32017R1001)
- EU Regulation recital 21: "the proprietor should not be entitled to prevent the fair and honest use of the EU trade mark for the purpose of identifying or referring to the goods or services as those of the proprietor." — [Regulation (EU) 2017/1001](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/1001/oj)
- Directive (EU) 2015/2436 Art. 14(1)(c): "the trade mark for the purpose of identifying or referring to goods or services as those of the proprietor of that trade mark, in particular, where the use of the trade mark is necessary to indicate the intended purpose of a product or service, in particular as accessories or spare parts." Art. 14(2): "Paragraph 1 shall only apply where the use made by the third party is in accordance with honest practices in industrial or commercial matters." Art. 14(3) covers earlier local rights. Recital 27 matches recital 21 above ("fair and honest use of the mark for the purpose of identifying or referring to the goods or services as those of the proprietor"). — [Directive (EU) 2015/2436](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2015/2436/oj) (CELEX 32015L2436)
- *BMW v Deenik*: a trade mark owner cannot stop a third party "from using the mark for the purpose of informing the public that he carries out the repair and maintenance of goods covered by that trade mark ... or that he has specialised or is a specialist in the sale or the repair and maintenance of such goods, unless the mark is used in a way that may create the impression that there is a commercial connection between the other undertaking and the trade mark proprietor". Also: "Whether advertising may create the impression that there is a commercial connection between the reseller and the trade mark proprietor is a question of fact for the national court to decide in the light of the circumstances of each case." — [C-63/97, 23 Feb 1999](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:61997CJ0063)
- *Gillette*: "the condition of 'honest use' ... constitutes in substance the expression of a duty to act fairly in relation to the legitimate interests of the trade mark owner". Use is not honest where "it is done in such a manner that it may give the impression that there is a commercial connection between the reseller and the trade mark proprietor"; where it "affect[s] the value of the trade mark by taking unfair advantage of its distinctive character or repute"; if "it discredits or denigrates that mark"; or where the third party presents its product as an "imitation or replica". — [C-228/03, 17 Mar 2005, paras. 41-45](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:62003CJ0228)

### Inferences
- A developer who names a former employer and its clients to describe their own past work is close to the *Welles* self-description pattern and the *Deenik* "specialist in BMW" pattern. Both are lawful if no affiliation or special relationship is implied.
- Across all three systems the same practical line appears: words to identify are fine; logos, stylized marks and official imagery are suspect; disclaimers help but are not required; claimed relationships must be true.
- Philippine Sec. 169.1 copies Lanham §43(a)(1) almost verbatim, so US false-endorsement reasoning is especially apt persuasive authority in the Philippines (inference; I found no Philippine case saying so).

### Gaps
- Other US circuits' nominative fair use approaches (e.g., Third Circuit *Century 21 v. LendingTree*, 425 F.3d 211; Second Circuit *Int'l Info. Sys. Sec. Certification Consortium v. Security University*, 823 F.3d 153) were not opened and are not verified here.
- No EU or US case specifically on client logos in portfolios was located.

## 6. Platform rules: Google Play and App Store badges (who may use them, linking, attribution, non-publishers) and the safest way for a non-publisher to link

### Takeaway
**Apple** licenses its badge only to the Apple Developer Program member for its own apps. An employee or ex-employee who did not publish an app has no licence to use the "Download on the App Store" badge for it, unless acting for the account holder with its permission.

**Google**'s current pages say badges "can be used in marketing campaigns to promote content on Google Play", and frame them for "App developers distributing an Android app". Google's own badge page as archived in June 2023 went further:
- "Any online use of the badge must link to the Google Play store";
- the badge was limited to content available on Google Play;
- any use of the Google Play name required an attribution footer.

The safest approach for a non-publisher is a plain text link ("View on Google Play" / "Available on the App Store") to the official listing, naming the actual publisher, with attribution lines, and with no badge artwork or Apple logo.

### Cited Findings
- On 2026-10-23, https://play.google.com/intl/en_us/badges/ returned a 302 redirect to the Partner Marketing Hub badge page (observed during this research).
- Current Google badge text: "Google Play badges can be used in marketing campaigns to promote content on Google Play. If you're driving customers to download content on Google Play, please use Google Play badges rather than Google Play lockups." "Get it on Google Play badge: Use this version to drive downloads on Google Play." "The badge height must be a minimum of 28px." "Don't change the badge color." "Don't remove or rearrange badge elements, or otherwise adjust the badge in any way." The page text I retrieved contains no linking requirement and no attribution line. — [Google Partner Marketing Hub, Google Play lockups, icons and badges](https://partnermarketinghub.withgoogle.com/brands/google-play/google-play/lockups-icons-badges/#badges)
- Google FAQ: "You don't need permission to use the Google Play badge in campaigns with fewer than 1 million estimated impressions." TV, out-of-home and campaigns over 1 million impressions "must still be submitted for approval" (via fetch tool). — [Partner Marketing Hub FAQ](https://partnermarketinghub.withgoogle.com/support/faq/)
- Android developer brand guidelines (page last updated 2026-08-07, via fetch tool): "App developers distributing an Android app can find the Google Play badge in the Partner Marketing Hub. This conflicts with the FAQ's under-1-million exemption. — [developer.android.com brand guidelines](https://developer.android.com/distribute/marketing-tools/brand-guidelines)

- Apple badge use: "Include App Store badges in all digital and printed marketing materials as a clear call to action to get your app." "Use one App Store badge per layout or video. Place the badge in a subordinate position to the image or main message so that it isn't the dominant artwork. Don't modify, angle, or animate the App Store badge." Don't use the standalone Apple logo." "Generate short links or embeddable code that lead to your App Store product page and display your app icon, a QR code, or an App Store badge." — [Apple App Store Marketing Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/marketing/guidelines/)
- Apple text and credit lines: "It is correct to say that an app is available on the App Store or can be downloaded from the App Store. Don't use at the App Store." - Apple's general third-party trademark rules:
  - "You may not use the Apple Logo or any other Apple-owned graphic symbol, logo, or icon on or in connection with web sites, products, packaging, manuals, promotional/advertising materials, or for any other purpose except pursuant to an express written trademark license from Apple".

  - Developers may use Apple word marks "in a referential phrase" where "The reference to Apple does not create a sense of endorsement, sponsorship, or false association with Apple or Apple products or services."
  - [Apple, Guidelines for Using Apple Trademarks and Copyrights](https://www.apple.com/legal/intellectual-property/guidelinesfor3rdparties.html)

### Inferences
- **Apple, LGU and DGSI apps.** The licensee "You" is whoever holds the developer account for the listing: each LGU, or DGSI for apps published under its account, including Eventbook Exhibitors. An individual employee cannot accept Apple's artwork licence for an account they do not represent. Using the badge on a personal portfolio for those apps is unlicensed unless the account holder authorises it, and after leaving, any such authority is doubtful.
- **Google, non-publisher.** Google's current text does not expressly limit badges to the publisher. It frames them for developers promoting their content, and its 2023 rules tied badge use to linking to the store and to attribution. A non-publisher using the badge risks a guideline breach and implies they are the publisher or that the app is "theirs" to promote. A text link avoids both.
- **Safest link pattern for non-publishers:** "Smile SAFE Taytay, published on Google Play and the App Store by [publisher as listed]: View on Google Play · Available on the App Store". Use plain hyperlinks to the official listing URLs, no badge images, no Apple logo, and a footer attribution: "Google Play and the Google Play logo are trademarks of Google LLC." and an Apple credit line in Apple's format.
- **The privately distributed responder app** has no public listing to link to. Text description only (confidentiality covered elsewhere).
- **Personal apps** the developer publishes under their own developer accounts may use official badges, following the size, no-modification, linking and attribution rules.

### Gaps
- The current Partner Marketing Hub text as retrieved does not state a linking requirement or attribution line, and I could not find a current Google legal-attribution page. Whether the 2023 requirements still apply is unconfirmed.
- The internal conflict between Google's FAQ (no permission under 1 million impressions) and the Android brand page (all creatives referencing Google trademarks must be approved) is unresolved.

## 7. Technology logos shown as skills via Simple Icons (React, Expo, TypeScript, Next.js, Tailwind, TanStack Query): the disclaimer and the projects' brand rules

### Takeaway
Simple Icons supplies the glyphs under CC0 for the project, but it disclaims trademark clearance and asks users to get permissions and follow brand guidelines. The brands differ:
- **Expo** expressly permits using its name and logos, less prominently than your own, to say you use Expo.
- **Vercel** permits truthful descriptive use and lets the Next.js symbol appear "in cases where only brand icons of multiple brands are displayed". It also requires an attribution statement and says "Do not use Vercel marks for commercial purposes", which bars them from "marketing collateral for your commercial products or services".
- **Meta**'s open-source policy allows the name but not "the stylized wording or the logo" under nominative use, and requires written permission for other uses.
- **TypeScript** bars using its logos "for your application/product" but calls its logo "right" for "general marketing for yourself".
- **Tailwind** bars implied affiliation and offers its assets for "articles and video tutorials".
- **TanStack** states only visual usage rules.

Several brands ban modifying their logos, colour changes included. A text skills list is the zero-risk option. A logo grid is widespread and low-risk if the logos are unmodified and subordinate to the developer's own name, and the page carries a not-affiliated notice and Vercel's attribution line.

### Cited Findings
- Simple Icons disclaimer:
  - "Simple Icons is released under CC0 - though that doesn't mean to imply that all icons within the project are also CC0. Please see individual licenses where available."
  - "Simple Icons cannot be held responsible for any legal activity raised by a brand, or users of the package. We ask that our users seek the correct permissions to use the icons relevant to their project."
  - "Simple Icons provides a link to a brand's _branding guidelines_ (or similar) if the brand provides one. We ask our users read these guidelines and ensure their usage of the brand's icon is in accordance with them."
  - "The lack of a `guidelines` entry for a particular brand does not imply that the brand has no guidelines."

- Meta (React):
  - "the right to use open source software does not include the right to related software or project names and logos."
  - Nominative fair use is allowed "so long as such use (1) cannot be readily identified without using the trademark (e.g., to reference an OS Trademark in order to truthfully state that a third party's software uses the source code of the OS Trademark); (2) includes the minimum necessary to identify the OS Trademark (e.g., the name may be used, but not the stylized wording or the logo); and (3) does not suggest any sponsorship, endorsement, or affiliation between the third party and Meta or the OS Trademarks."

### Inferences
- A "Skills" grid of small, unmodified logos labelled with the technology names is a referential use telling visitors which tools the developer works with:
  - It matches Expo's expressly permitted use ("describe that you are using an Expo product") and Vercel's allowance of the Next.js symbol among "brand icons of multiple brands".
  - It sits uneasily with Meta's policy, which excludes logos from nominative use, and with Vercel's "no commercial purposes / marketing collateral" line, given the site's Services page.
  - The practical risk is low, because these communities widely display such grids, but strict compliance points to plain text names or permission for React and Next.js.
- **Recolouring breaches the rules.** Simple Icons glyphs are single-colour and often re-tinted to match a site theme. That is a "modification" under Expo's rule ("including changing the color") and Vercel's "Do not modify the Vercel marks". Use official colours or text labels.
- **Required and advisable notices:** Vercel's attribution sentence (mandatory under Vercel's rules whenever its marks are used), plus a general line such as "Technology names and logos are trademarks of their respective owners; no affiliation or endorsement is implied."
- **Keep the tech logos subordinate** to the developer's own name (Expo, Vercel) and away from wording like "Partners" or "Certified".

### Gaps
- No React-specific brand guideline beyond Meta's general open-source policy was found.
- Microsoft's general trademark guidelines (which may govern TypeScript) were not checked.
- TanStack's trademark status and policy beyond the brand guide were not found.
- Individual Simple Icons entries' "license" and "guidelines" fields for these six icons were not checked.
