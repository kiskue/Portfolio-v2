# Copyright ownership, fair use and moral rights: showing employer and client software work in a Philippine developer's portfolio (with US, EU and UK comparators)

Researched 2026-10-23. Scope: copyright and moral rights only. Trademarks and brand badges, confidentiality and trade secrets, privacy and government security, and industry practice are covered in the sibling notes in this folder. **How to read the statute:** lawphil's RA 8293 page reproduces the **original 1997 text** without amendments ([RA 8293, lawphil](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)). The 2013 amendments have to be read from RA 10372 itself ([RA 10372, lawphil](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html)). Every RA 8293 quotation below was taken from the lawphil page, and every amendment from the full RA 10372 text. A provision is marked "(unamended)" only where the full text of RA 10372 shows that the section was not touched.

## 1. What the current statute says: RA 8293 Part IV as amended by RA 10372 (2013), and whether any later law changed it

### Takeaway
The copyright part of RA 8293 has been amended only once, by RA 10372 (approved 28 Feb 2013). For this question, RA 10372 changed Sec. 171.3 (communication to the public), Sec. 171.9 (reproduction), Sec. 180 (assignment or licence must be in writing), Sec. 181, Sec. 185.1 (fair use and decompilation), Sec. 198 (term of moral rights) and Sec. 216 (infringement and statutory damages). It left Secs. 172, 173, 175, 176, 177, 178, 193, 195 and 196 exactly as enacted in 1997. I found no later amending law. As of 29 Sept 2026 the bills to strengthen online enforcement and site blocking (SB 1708, SB 959, HB 4702, HB 4773, HB 5944) were still pending in committee, so they are **not law**.

### Inferences
- Many commentaries quote the 1997 text, so two amended provisions are easy to misquote. First, Sec. 180.2 now covers **licences as well as assignments**: neither is "deemed" made without a written indication. Second, Sec. 198.1 now makes the **attribution right (193.1) perpetual**, and keeps all moral rights non-assignable.
- Sec. 172.1 lists computer programs (n), drawings and plans of a technical or scientific character (i), (j), and pictorial illustrations (m). So code, UI artwork, icons, store screenshots and architecture drawings are all protectable "works" from creation (Sec. 172.2). Sec. 175 still excludes the underlying "system, method or operation" and "mere data".
- The IP Code is a "special law" (see Gozon, Q7). On its text, RA 10175 Sec. 6 could raise the penalty by one degree when infringement is committed through ICT, such as a website. I found no case applying Sec. 6 to IP Code offences, so this is untested.

### Gaps
- I did not check the Official Gazette or the Senate and House trackers directly for laws enacted between Sept and Oct 2026. The "no later amendment" conclusion rests on the 29 Sept 2026 Asia IP report and on finding no enacted amendment in searches.
- No consolidated official text of RA 8293 as amended was opened. IPOPHL publishes one, but its pages would not render for the fetch tool, so the consolidation above is my own.

## 2. Who owns the code and UI: employee on regular duties vs not, contractor or commissioned work, and the effect of an IP-assignment clause

### Takeaway
Code, UI, icons and store graphics that a DGSI employee produced as part of **regularly-assigned duties** belong to the **employer** by law (Sec. 178.3(b)), unless there is an express or implied agreement otherwise. Works outside regular duties belong to the **employee**, even if made on company time or equipment (Sec. 178.3(a)). For **commissioned** work done by a paid non-employee, the commissioning party owns "the work" but the copyright **stays with the creator** unless there is a *written* stipulation (Sec. 178.4; *Republic v. Heirs of Tupaz*, 2020). Any assignment or licence must be shown in writing (Sec. 180.2 as amended). So an IP-assignment clause mainly matters for (a) side projects outside regular duties and (b) contractor work. For LGU apps there is a second layer: DGSI's copyright may itself have been transferred to the LGU by the procurement contract. Standard government consulting contracts make software the "sole and exclusive property" of the procuring entity.

### Cited Findings
- The statutory rules in Sec. 178.1, 178.2, 178.3(a)/(b) and 178.4 are quoted in Q1 — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- *Republic of the Philippines (through the PNP) v. Heirs of Jose C. Tupaz IV*, G.R. No. 197335, 7 Sept 2020 (Third Division, Leonen, J.), restates the RA 8293 rules: "Section 178.3 of Republic Act No. 8293, retains the rule regarding works created in the course of the employment." It also holds: "The rule regarding commissioned works is modified under Republic Act No. 8293. Parties no longer have joint ownership over the copyright. Under Section 178.4 of Republic Act No. 8293, the copyright of a commissioned work generally belongs to the creator. However, the parties may agree in writing to transfer the copyright to the person who commissioned the work." — [SC E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67242)
- *Tupaz* on what counts as "commissioned": the work must be paid for. "The creator of the work should be paid valuable consideration for the work made." The PNP lost because "petitioner did not hire respondent Tupaz's services for a fee or a commission. Respondent Tupaz rendered his services voluntarily", and "there was no employer-employee relationship between the parties at the time the designs were made." The Court added: "Petitioner could have avoided this dispute had it entered into a contract that clearly and expressly spelled out the extent of each party's rights". Disposition: "the Petition for Review is DENIED", affirming the CA, which had upheld Tupaz's copyright registrations — [SC E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67242)
- *Tupaz* on authorship: "The copyright of a derivative work solely belongs to the person who fixes an idea into a tangible medium of expression. The law on copyright only protects the expression of an idea, not the idea itself. Thus, one who merely contributes concepts or ideas is not deemed an author." Also: "a creator or an author pertains to someone who transforms an abstract idea into a tangible form of expression through the application of skill or labor." — [SC E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67242)
- Under Sec. 180.2 as amended, copyright "is not deemed assigned or licensed inter vivos, in whole or in part, unless there is a written indication of such intention." Under Sec. 181 as amended, copyright "is distinct from the property in the material object" — [RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html)
- Works of the Government: no copyright subsists in a "work of the Government", which is limited to works created by a government "officer or employee ... as a part of his regularly prescribed official duties" (Secs. 171.11, 176.1). The Government may "receiv[e] and hold[] copyrights transferred to it by assignment" (Sec. 176.3) — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- A template government consulting contract assigns software to the agency. The Philippine Bidding Documents for Consulting Services (Third Edition, Oct 2009, DPWH copy), GCC 35.1: "All plans, drawings, specifications, designs, reports, other documents and software prepared by the Consultant for the Procuring Entity under this Contract shall become and remain the property of the Procuring Entity ... The Consultant may retain a copy of such documents and software." GCC 35.2: "All computer programs developed by the Consultant under this Contract shall be the sole and exclusive property of the Procuring Entity; provided, however, that the Consultant may use such programs for its own use with prior written approval of the Procuring Entity." — [PBD Consulting Services, DPWH](https://dpwh.gov.ph/DPWH/sites/default/files/pdf/business/procurement/consultancy/PBD.CONS.pdf)
- A search summary says IPOPHL's Copyright FAQ notes that different rules apply to works made in employment or on commission. The page did not render for me, so its wording is **not verified** — [IPOPHL Copyright FAQs](https://www.ipophil.gov.ph/help-and-support/copyright/)

### Inferences
- **DGSI public-safety apps, Eventbook Exhibitors, and ERP, telemedicine and virtual-ID work at an earlier job.** For an employee developer hired to build these products, the work is almost certainly "the result of the performance of his regularly-assigned duties". The **employer** therefore owned the copyright from creation, whether or not a contract was signed (178.3(b)). The developer remains the **author** (171.1; *Tupaz*), which matters for moral rights (Q3), but not the owner. The portfolio FAQ line that "company code is proprietary" matches this.
- **LGU white-label apps.** If DGSI built them for paying LGUs, then as between DGSI and the LGU the default under 178.4 is that DGSI keeps the copyright and the LGU owns "the work". That default flips if the procurement contract contains a written stipulation. The PBD consulting template's "sole and exclusive property" wording would very likely count as one. If DGSI's contracts follow that template, the LGU may hold the rights, and **even DGSI needs the LGU's "prior written approval" to use the programs for its own purposes**. Portfolio permission may therefore have to come from the LGU as well as DGSI. The actual DGSI–LGU contracts, procurement mode and PBD edition are unknown, and the contracts may differ (for example, a SaaS or licence model where DGSI keeps the IP). The `com.dgsi.*` package names suggest DGSI runs the store listings but do not prove who owns the copyright.
- The apps are **not "works of the Government"**, because DGSI staff are not government officers or employees (171.11). They are therefore protected by copyright, whoever owns it. LGU seals and logos made by LGU employees in their official duties would be works of the Government with no copyright. But 176.1 requires the agency's "prior approval ... for exploitation of such work for profit". Whether showing a seal on a portfolio with a Services page counts as "exploitation for profit" is untested, and seals are also governed by non-copyright rules (see the trademarks and government sibling notes).
- **Variation matrix (copyright ownership only):**

| Scenario | Who owns copyright in the code and UI | Effect on portfolio use |
|---|---|---|
| Employee, work within regularly-assigned duties, no written contract | Employer (178.3(b)) | Needs permission or fair use (Q4). The developer is still the author. |
| Same, plus an employment contract with an IP-assignment clause | Employer (the clause confirms it) | As above. The contract may add confidentiality or publicity limits, enforceable as contract obligations. |
| Employee, work **outside** regular duties (side or personal project), even using employer time or equipment | Employee (178.3(a)), unless there is a written assignment (180.2) | The developer can show it freely, unless a valid written assignment covers it. |
| Contractor or freelancer, paid, no written IP stipulation | Creator keeps copyright; client owns "the work" (178.4; *Tupaz*) | The developer may reproduce their own code and UI, subject to any confidentiality terms. |
| Contractor with written assignment, or a "work belongs to client" clause | Client (178.4 written stipulation; 180.1) | Needs the client's written licence (180.2), such as a portfolio clause or an email. |
| Unpaid volunteer, no employment (the *Tupaz* facts) | Creator | Free to show. |
| Current vs **former** employee | No change: ownership is fixed at creation | Same copyright analysis. Only contract or NDA terms change with exit. |
| With vs without NDA | No change to copyright ownership | An NDA adds separate contract liability for disclosing confidential material, even if copyright would allow the use. |

- Written permission matters in practice. Because 180.2 requires a "written indication" before a licence is "deemed" granted, an oral "sure, you can show it" from a manager is weaker in the Philippines than under US implied-licence doctrine. An email from someone with authority is the cheap fix. This is my reading of the 2013 text; I found no case on it.

### Gaps
- I found no Supreme Court decision applying 178.3 to software, or defining "regularly-assigned duties". The developer's job description would be the natural evidence; a search snippet suggests IPOPHL applies that approach to government "official duties", but I did **not verify** this.
- I did not determine whether an employment-contract clause assigning *future* side-project IP (overriding 178.3(a)) is enforceable under Philippine civil or labour law.
- The actual DGSI–LGU contract terms are unknown. I also did not verify whether current (post-RA 12009, 2024) PBD editions keep GCC 35.1–35.2. The DPWH copy is the 2009 edition.

## 3. Moral rights: may the developer say "I built this"? Effect of Sec. 195 (waiver), Sec. 196 (collective works) and Sec. 198 (term)

### Takeaway
In the Philippines an employee developer is the **author** of the code and UI they wrote, and keeps moral rights "independently of the economic rights ... or the grant of an assignment" (Sec. 193). The rights cannot be assigned or licensed, and the attribution right now lasts in perpetuity (Sec. 198.1 as amended). Two things weaken the right to *require* credit. It can be waived in writing, with limits (Sec. 195). And for a contribution to a **collective work** (Sec. 171.2) it is **deemed waived unless expressly reserved** (Sec. 196). Neither provision, nor anything else in the copyright statute, forbids the author from truthfully stating "I built this". Restrictions on saying so would have to come from contract or confidentiality law, not copyright. The UK and US give software authors much less (Q8).

### Cited Findings
- The texts of Sec. 193.1–193.4, 195, 196 and 197 (unamended) are quoted in Q1 — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Under Sec. 198.1 as amended by RA 10372 Sec. 17, the 193.1 right lasts "during the lifetime of the author and in perpetuity after his death", the other moral rights are "coterminous with the economic rights", and "the moral rights shall not be assignable or subject to license" — [RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html)
- Sec. 171.1 defines the "Author" as "the natural person who has created the work". The collective-work definition in Sec. 171.2 requires creation "at the initiative and under the direction of another with the understanding that it will be disclosed by the latter under his own name and that contributing natural persons will not be identified" — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Sec. 199: moral-rights violations carry "the same rights and remedies available to a copyright owner", plus Civil Code damages. Criminal liability in Sec. 217.1 covers "any right secured by provisions of Part IV", and Part IV includes the moral-rights chapter — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- *Tupaz*: "one who merely contributes concepts or ideas is not deemed an author." — [SC E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67242)
- Berne Art. 6bis(1) is the treaty source: "Independently of the author's economic rights, and even after the transfer of the said rights, the author shall have the right to claim authorship of the work and to object to any distortion, mutilation or other modification of, or other derogatory action in relation to, the said work, which would be prejudicial to his honor or reputation." — [Berne (Paris 1971) Art. 6bis, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/treaties/berne/6bis.html)

### Inferences
- **Authorship survives employer ownership.** Sec. 178.3(b) gives the employer the *copyright* but does not make it the *author*. Sec. 193 rights belong to "the author ... independently of ... assignment". Unlike the US work-made-for-hire rule (Q8), Philippine law does not deem the employer to be the author, so the developer holds moral rights in their code and UI.
- **"I built this" is a factual claim, not a use of the work.** Truthfully stating one's role, or listing app names, does not reproduce, adapt, display or communicate the employer's work, so it does not touch the 177 economic rights. Sec. 196 waives only the author's "right to have his contribution attributed to him", meaning the right to *make others credit him*. It does not create a duty of silence. Contractual confidentiality (if any) is the real limit.
- **A team-built white-label app probably qualifies as a "collective work".** It is built by several developers under DGSI's direction and released under DGSI or LGU branding with no individual credits. If so, under Sec. 196 the developer could not *force* DGSI or the LGU to credit them in the app or listing unless they had "expressly reserve[d]" it. A written waiver in an employment contract (Sec. 195) would have the same effect, but a waiver cannot validly allow use of the author's name for a work he did not create (195.2).
- **Accuracy duties run both ways in an architecture diagram that says "who built each part".** Co-workers are authors of their parts, with 193.1 and 193.4 rights to be credited and to stop their names being attached to work they did not create. Under *Tupaz*, someone who only gave ideas or specifications to a part is not its author, so labels like "built by me" for parts the developer only specified overstate authorship. Careful wording such as "designed", "led" or "implemented" lowers both moral-rights and misrepresentation risk.
- **Unpublished apps** (the privately distributed responder app and the R&D push-to-talk prototype). Sec. 193.2 gives each *author* the right "to withhold it from publication". The *economic* right to communicate the work belongs to the employer, so neither side can unilaterally publish screenshots. This is a further reason to avoid showing unpublished internal software.

### Gaps
- I found no Philippine case or IPOPHL opinion on employee moral rights in software, on whether a commercial app is a "collective work", or on the validity of blanket moral-rights waivers in employment contracts.
- Whether 199 and 217 have ever been used to prosecute misattribution is unknown.

## 4. Is reproducing UI screenshots, store screenshots and app icons on a personal portfolio infringement, or fair use under Sec. 185?

### Takeaway
Re-hosting official store screenshots and app icons, or posting one's own screenshots of the apps, on a portfolio server *prima facie* uses the owner's exclusive rights of reproduction (177.1, with "temporary or permanent, in whole or in part" copies under 171.9 as amended), public display (177.5) and communication to the public (177.7). Without a written licence (180.2), the developer must rely on **fair use (Sec. 185)**. That defence is **arguable but uncertain**, and there is no Philippine case on portfolio use. Factors 2 and 4 favour the developer: the works are published and functional, and there is no market substitution. Factor 1 cuts against: *ABS-CBN v. Gozon* says the purpose "must fall under those listed" and that "commercial use ... can be weighed against fair use", and a site with a Services page is commercial self-promotion. Factor 3 is mixed. Fair use is "a matter of defense", and infringement is *malum prohibitum*. The legally clean route is the owner's written permission.

### Cited Findings
- The texts of Sec. 177.1, 177.5, 177.7 and 171.9 as amended are quoted in Q1 — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html); [RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html)
- *ABS-CBN Corp. v. Gozon*, G.R. No. 195956, 11 Mar 2015 (Second Division, Leonen, J.), on factor 1: "First, the purpose and character of the use of the copyrighted material must fall under those listed in Section 185, thus: 'criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching including multiple copies for classroom use, scholarship, research, and similar purposes.' The purpose and character requirement is important in view of copyright's goal to promote creativity and encourage creation of works. Hence, commercial use of the copyrighted work can be weighed against fair use." — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2015/mar2015/gr_195956_2015.html)
- *Gozon* on transformative use and meta-use: "The 'transformative test' is generally used in reviewing the purpose and character of the usage of the copyrighted work. This court must look into whether the copy of the work adds 'new expression, meaning or message' to transform it into something else. 'Meta-use' can also occur without necessarily transforming the copyrighted work used." — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2015/mar2015/gr_195956_2015.html)
- *Gozon* on factors 2 to 4: "If the nature of the work is more factual than creative, then fair use will be weighed in favor of the user." "An exact reproduction of a copyrighted work, compared to a small portion of it, can result in the conclusion that its use is not fair. There may also be cases where, though the entirety of the copyrighted work is used without consent, its purpose determines that the usage is still fair." "If this court finds that the use had or will have a negative impact on the copyrighted work's market, then the use is deemed unfair." — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2015/mar2015/gr_195956_2015.html)
- *Gozon*'s definition of fair use: "a privilege to use the copyrighted material in a reasonable manner without the consent of the copyright owner or as copying the theme or ideas rather than their expression." The Court also held: "Whether the alleged five-second footage may be considered fair use is a matter of defense. ... Raising the defense of fair use does not automatically mean that no infringement was committed." Disposition: "the Petition is partially GRANTED". The DOJ resolution ordering an information to be filed was reinstated against two GMA-7 officers, its Head of News and Public Affairs and a Program Manager. — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2015/mar2015/gr_195956_2015.html)
- *Gozon* on intent: "unless clearly provided in the law, offenses involving infringement of copyright protections should be considered malum prohibitum. It is the act of infringement, not the intent, which causes the damage." — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2015/mar2015/gr_195956_2015.html)
- *Philippine Home Cable Holdings v. FILSCAP*, G.R. No. 188933, 21 Feb 2023 (En Banc, Leonen, SAJ), sets the elements of infringement: "To uphold a copyright infringement claim, the following must be proved: first, the complainant or plaintiff's ownership of a validly copyrighted material, and second, the defendant or respondent's exercise of any the enumerated economic rights without the consent of the copyright owner or holder." — [ChanRobles reproduction](https://chanrobles.com/cralaw/2023februarydecisions.php?id=137)
- Exposure: statutory damages of at least PHP 50,000, reducible to at most PHP 10,000 for an infringer who "was not aware and had no reason to believe" (216.1 as amended). Criminal penalty for a first offence: 1–3 years plus PHP 50,000–150,000 (217.1(a)) — [RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html); [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- The quotation exception (184.1(b)) requires the use to be "compatible with fair use and only to the extent justified for the purpose" and that "the source and the name of the author, if appearing on the work, are mentioned". 184.2 adds the three-step "normal exploitation / legitimate interests" test — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- IPOPHL issued "statutory fair use guidelines" in March 2024, covering Sec. 184 (specific acts) and Sec. 185 ("Section 185 of the IP Code contains the general concept of fair use"). The secondary report gives no circular number and does not address commercial or online use. I did not review the guidelines themselves — [Asia IP, 7 Mar 2024](https://asiaiplaw.com/section/news-analysis/ipophl-releases-statutory-fair-use-guidelines-to-clarify-rules-on-copyright-exceptions); IPOPHL's own release did not render: [IPOPHL news](https://ipophil.gov.ph/news/ipophl-releases-statutory-fair-use-guidelines-to-clarify-rules-on-copyright-exceptions)
- Names are not copyright subject matter. *Kho v. CA*, G.R. No. 115758, 19 Mar 2002: "Trademark, copyright and patents are different intellectual property rights that cannot be interchanged with one another. ... the scope of a copyright is confined to literary and artistic works which are original intellectual creations in the literary and artistic domain". The Court held that a product's name and container are trademark, not copyright, subjects — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2002/mar2002/gr_115758_2002.html)
- Facts and data are excluded: Sec. 175 denies protection to "mere data as such" — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Persuasive US analogue, from a secondary source only: *Neri v. Monroe*, No. 11-CV-429-SLC, 2014 WL 793336 (W.D. Wis. Feb. 26, 2014). A design firm's photos of a remodel containing a commissioned glass sculpture, used for industry award applications and on the firm's website, were held fair use. The court found the firm's purpose of publishing its own work proper and the copying incidental, and found no market harm because the artist had never licensed derivative works. Factor 2 favoured the artist. I did not open the opinion — [CDAS summary](https://cdas.com/photo-glass-sculpture-integrated-ceiling-fair-use/)
- US practitioner view (Linda Joy Kattwinkel, Graphic Artists Guild, © 2004): "reproducing your own work in your portfolio (whether in print or online) is fair use, regardless of who owns the copyright", and "the copyright owners rarely object". She adds that the employer "may want public credit for the work itself" and "[t]he safest practice is to explicitly address this issue in your agreements". This is opinion, not a holding — [Graphic Artists Guild](https://graphicartistsguild.org/fair-use-or-infringement/)

### Inferences
**Four-factor analysis for this developer under Sec. 185, as applied by Gozon:**
- **(a) Purpose and character: leans against, but contestable.** The use is self-promotion to get hired and to win paid freelance work, with a Services page on the same site, so it is commercial. Self-promotion is not one of the listed purposes, and *Gozon* says the purpose "must fall under those listed" (though the list ends "and similar purposes"). On the other side, the screenshot is used as **evidence of the developer's own past work**, not for what the app does for citizens or to market the app. That is arguably a different "meaning or message", or at least a *Gozon* "meta-use", like *Neri v. Monroe*. Adding real commentary (role, problem solved, decisions made) strengthens the "comment" and "quotation" framing. Bare galleries weaken it.
- **(b) Nature of the work: favours the developer for the public apps.** Store screenshots and functional UI are published (171.7) and largely utilitarian, closer to "factual than creative" in *Gozon*'s terms. For the **unpublished** responder app and R&D prototype, 185.2 still allows fair use, but unpublished status, confidentiality, the authors' 193.2 right to withhold, and the employer's interest all weigh heavily against.
- **(c) Amount: mixed.** A few screens are a small part of an app with many screens. But each store screenshot and each icon is a complete work in itself. Small, low-resolution images reduce the taking.
- **(d) Market effect: favours the developer.** The citizen apps are free, a portfolio image does not replace downloading the app, and there is no obvious licensing market for portfolio screenshots. Showing them next to a store link may even promote the app. Harm from disclosing confidential information or implying endorsement is not a *copyright* market harm, though contract or trademark law may capture it.
- **Net:** for *published* app screens and store images used sparingly with commentary, fair use is a reasonable but untested argument in the Philippines. For *unpublished* internal apps it is weak. Exposure is real but modest: statutory damages floors, possible criminal liability where intent is irrelevant (*malum prohibitum*), and possibly a higher penalty if RA 10175 Sec. 6 applies. In practice, enforcement against a former employee's portfolio is usually a takedown request. That is my inference; I found no data.
- **App icons:** these are artistic works (172.1(g)/(m)) and usually also trademarks. The copyright analysis follows the screenshots: the whole icon is copied, but it is used to *identify* the app. The trademark side is covered in the sibling note.
- **Company, client, LGU and app names; metrics:** names are outside copyright (*Kho*), and metrics are "mere data" (175). Copyright is not the obstacle here; confidentiality and trademark are.
- **Technology logos shown as skills** are artistic works and trademarks. The copyright risk is negligible when vendors' published brand assets are used under their terms; see the trademark sibling note.
- **Permission route:** a written licence (an email suffices as a "written indication", per my reading of 180.2) from the copyright owner removes the copyright issue entirely. For LGU apps, that may mean both DGSI and the LGU (Q2).

### Gaps
- No Philippine decision addresses portfolio use, screenshots of software, or self-promotional use under Sec. 185. *Gozon* involved news footage and was decided at the probable-cause stage.
- I did not read the text of IPOPHL's 2024 fair-use guidelines, so any IPOPHL position on online, commercial or portfolio use is unknown.
- *Neri v. Monroe* was verified only through a law-firm summary, which itself contains a court-name inconsistency (it says "Western District of Washington" in the text but cites W.D. Wis.).
- I did not verify the US Supreme Court's *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* (2023) treatment of commercial purpose under factor 1. It would be relevant to how persuasive US analogies remain.

## 5. Re-created ("replica") screens with fictional data, UI "look and feel", and self-drawn architecture diagrams

### Takeaway
Philippine law has no statute provision or reported case on GUI or "look and feel" protection. The governing rules are the idea/expression line (Sec. 175; *Joaquin v. Drilon*; *Tupaz*) and the reproduction and adaptation rights (177.1 "substantial portion", 177.2 "adaptation ... or other transformation", 173.2). A replica rebuilt from scratch can still infringe if it reproduces the original UI's **protected visual expression**: distinctive graphics, icons, illustrations, and the specific selection and arrangement of screens, layout, text and colours. Rebuilding with new code and fictional data does not cure that. A replica that conveys only the functions, flows and generic patterns in the developer's own visual design copies unprotected ideas and systems. Self-drawn architecture diagrams are the developer's own works, and the architecture itself is an unprotected "system, method or operation". The risks there are confidentiality and security, not copyright.

### Cited Findings
- Sec. 175 excludes "any idea, procedure, system, method or operation, concept, principle, discovery or mere data as such, even if they are expressed, explained, illustrated or embodied in a work" — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- The texts of 177.1 ("Reproduction of the work or substantial portion of the work"), 177.2 ("adaptation ... or other transformation") and 173.2 (a derivative work does not "imply any right to such use of the original works") are quoted in Q1 — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- Reproduction covers copies "in whole or in part ... in any manner or form" (171.9 as amended) — [RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html)
- *Joaquin v. Drilon*, G.R. No. 108946, 28 Jan 1999: "The format or mechanics of a television show is not included in the list of protected works ... For this reason, the protection afforded by the law cannot be extended to cover them." Also: "The copyright does not extend to an idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery, regardless of the form in which it is described, explained, illustrated, or embodied in such work." And: "Copyright, in the strict sense of the term, is purely a statutory right." — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1999/jan1999/gr_108946_1999.html)
- *Pearl & Dean (Phil.) v. Shoemart*, G.R. No. 148222, 15 Aug 2003: copyright "can cover only the works falling within the statutory enumeration or description". A registration covering technical drawings did not extend to the light boxes built from them ("what was copyrighted were the technical drawings only, and not the light boxes themselves"). That is the CA's ruling, quoted by the SC, which affirmed the CA decision "in toto". — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2003/aug2003/gr_148222_2003.html)
- *Habana v. Robles*, G.R. No. 131522, 19 July 1999, on the substantiality test: "To constitute infringement, it is not necessary that the whole or even a large portion of the work shall have been copied. If so much is taken that the value of the original is sensibly diminished, or the labors of the original author are substantially and to an injurious extent appropriated by another, that is sufficient in point of law to constitute piracy." The Court also cautioned: "similarity of the allegedly infringed work to the author's or proprietor's copyrighted work does not of itself establish copyright infringement, especially if the similarity results from the fact that both works deal with the same subject or have the same common source" (the Court was quoting the CA's reasoning, which it went on to reverse) — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1999/jul1999/gr_131522_1999.html)
- *Tupaz*: "ideas are not protectable" and "To create a thing that may be entitled to a copyright requires something more than the giving of ideas and concepts." — [SC E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67242)
- EU analogue, *BSA v Ministerstvo kultury* (C-393/09, 22 Dec 2010): a GUI "is not a form of expression of a computer program ... Nevertheless, such an interface can be protected by copyright as a work ... if that interface is its author's own intellectual creation." Also: "where the expression of those components is dictated by their technical function, the criterion of originality is not met, since the different methods of implementing an idea are so limited that the idea and the expression become indissociable." (paras 49–51 and ruling) — [CJEU, EU Publications Office](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/62009CJ0393)
- EU Software Directive Art. 1(2): "Ideas and principles which underlie any element of a computer program, including those which underlie its interfaces, are not protected by copyright under this Directive." — [Directive 2009/24/EC Art. 1, legislation.gov.uk (EU text archive)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2009/24/article/1)

### Inferences
- **Replica screens are judged by what they copy, not how they were made.** Copying "in any manner or form" (171.9) includes redrawing by hand or rebuilding in code. If the replica reproduces the original's distinctive visual layer — its illustrations, icon set, characteristic layouts, microcopy and colour-plus-arrangement — closely enough that a "substantial portion" of its expression is taken (*Habana*), it is a reproduction or an unauthorized adaptation (177.1/177.2), and 173.2 gives the replica-maker no right to use the original. Using fictional data removes privacy concerns, and the original's data was unprotected anyway (175), but the visual expression remains.
- **Lower-risk replica design.** Show the *features and flows* (an SOS button, an incident-report form, a map of responders, a push-to-talk screen) in the developer's own visual language, with neutral sample branding. These are ideas, systems and methods of operation (175; *Joaquin*), and many components are dictated by function (the *BSA* reasoning would deny them originality). Avoid copying LGU seals, logos and brand palettes. Colours alone are not a copyright problem, but the overall "get-up" and official insignia raise trademark and government-insignia issues covered in the sibling notes. Label the screens "concept re-creation, not the production app". That helps with misrepresentation concerns, although it does not affect the copyright analysis.
- **No Philippine look-and-feel doctrine.** Philippine courts often draw on US and foreign reasoning (*Gozon*, *Joaquin* and *Habana* all cite US authority). A court would most likely apply the idea/expression dichotomy and the substantial-similarity test above rather than protect "look and feel" as such.
- **Architecture diagrams.** A diagram the developer draws now, outside their DGSI duties, is their own work (172.1(i)/(j); 178.3(a)). The *system* it depicts (components, data flows, which service talks to which) is a "system, method or operation" with no copyright (175). By analogy with *Pearl & Dean*, copyright in a drawing and the thing depicted are distinct. Copying or tracing an **internal DGSI diagram** would reproduce the employer's work (178.3(b)). For public-safety systems, the real risks are confidentiality, trade secrets and security exposure (sibling notes), plus per-part attribution accuracy (Q3).

### Gaps
- I found no Philippine case on software UI, screen displays, clones, or substantial similarity in software. The Philippine law-firm articles on "look and feel" that turned up (ndvlaw.com) returned HTTP 403, and search snippets suggested they cite no Philippine GUI case.
- I did not verify US look-and-feel authorities (*Lotus v. Borland*, *Apple v. Microsoft*, *Tetris v. Xio*) in this session, so they are not cited.

## 6. Is hyperlinking to public Google Play and App Store listings a copyright issue?

### Takeaway
On a Philippine reading, linking to the owner's own public store listing does not reproduce anything. It sends no copy of the work and makes nothing available beyond what the owner already made available to everyone. No Philippine statute provision, case or IPOPHL opinion addresses hyperlinks. The "communication to the public" right (171.3 as amended, 177.7) has been interpreted by the Supreme Court only for cable retransmission (*Home Cable*, 2023). Foreign authority points the same way. The CJEU in *Svensson* held that links to freely available works reach no "new public". *GS Media* raises the risk only for links to *unauthorized* content posted for profit. The US Ninth Circuit's server test (*Perfect 10*, reaffirmed in *Hunley*, 2023) holds that linking or embedding without storing a copy is not a "display". Copyright risk from store links is therefore negligible. Store badge graphics are trademarks with their own rules (sibling note).

### Cited Findings
- The amended Sec. 171.3 definition includes "the making of a work available to the public by wire or wireless means in such a way that members of the public may access these works from a place and time individually chosen by them" — [RA 10372](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10372_2013.html)
- *Philippine Home Cable Holdings v. FILSCAP*, G.R. No. 188933, 21 Feb 2023 (En Banc): "When a cable television system operator transmits a musical composition fixed in an audiovisual derivative work over a channel they control and operate, the operator is making that work accessible to members of the public from a place or time individually chosen by them." This is the only Supreme Court treatment of the "communication to the public" right I found, and it involves actual transmission, not linking — [ChanRobles reproduction](https://chanrobles.com/cralaw/2023februarydecisions.php?id=137)
- *Svensson* (C-466/12, 13 Feb 2014), ruling: "the provision on a website of clickable links to works freely available on another website does not constitute an 'act of communication to the public'". On "new public" (para 26): "The public targeted by the initial communication consisted of all potential visitors to the site concerned, since, given that access to the works on that site was not subject to any restrictive measures, all Internet users could therefore have free access to them." Para 28: "since there is no new public, the authorisation of the copyright holders is not required". Para 29: the outcome does not change if "the work appears in such a way as to give the impression that it is appearing on the site on which that link is found". Para 31: links that "circumvent restrictions put in place by the site ... in order to restrict public access" reach a new public — [CJEU, EU Publications Office](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/62012CJ0466)
- *GS Media* (C-160/15, 8 Sept 2016), ruling: for links "to protected works, which are freely available on another website without the consent of the copyright holder", the question is "whether those links are provided without the pursuit of financial gain by a person who did not know or could not reasonably have known the illegal nature of the publication ... or whether, on the contrary, those links are provided for such a purpose, a situation in which that knowledge must be presumed." — [CJEU, EU Publications Office](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/62015CJ0160)
- *Hunley v. Instagram*, No. 22-15293 (9th Cir. 17 July 2023): "We agree with the district court that Perfect 10 forecloses relief in this case." Describing *Perfect 10 v. Amazon*, 508 F.3d 1146 (9th Cir. 2007): "a person displays a photographic image by using a computer to fill a computer screen with a copy of the photographic image fixed in the computer's memory", a requirement "known as the 'Server Test.'" Without a copy on its servers, "Google transmits or communicates only an address which directs a user's browser to the location where a copy of the full-size image is displayed. Google does not communicate a display of the work itself." Also: "the embedding website does not store a copy of the underlying image." — [9th Cir. opinion PDF](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2023/07/17/22-15293.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Public store links are low risk.** The listings are published by the right-holder (DGSI or the LGU) and open to "all Internet users". A link reaches no public the owner did not already target (*Svensson* paras 26–28). Nothing on the portfolio server is copied, so under the US server test there is no display. GS Media's presumption does not apply because the content is authorized.
- **Do not link to private distribution channels.** For the privately distributed responder app, a link to a private download page, enterprise-distribution URL or test track would be the kind of link that "circumvent[s] restrictions" (*Svensson* para 31). It could also breach confidentiality.
- **Hot-linking or embedding store images vs re-hosting them.** Re-hosting creates copies (171.9: "temporary or permanent"). Embedding images served from the store's CDN avoids a copy on the portfolio server, and under *Hunley*/*Perfect 10* would not be a US "display". Philippine law has no server-test precedent, and the store's terms may prohibit hot-linking (not checked), so this is a mitigation, not a safe harbour.

### Gaps
- I found no Philippine case or IPOPHL opinion on hyperlinking, embedding or framing.
- Later CJEU framing and embedding cases (for example *VG Bild-Kunst*, C-392/19) were not verified.
- I did not open the original *Perfect 10* opinion; its text is quoted here via *Hunley*.

## 7. Philippine Supreme Court decisions and IPOPHL opinions relevant to employee works, software, screenshots or portfolio use

### Takeaway
No Supreme Court decision or IPOPHL opinion addresses portfolio display, software screenshots or UI protection. The relevant body of law is *Tupaz* (2020) on ownership of employee and commissioned works and on authorship, *Gozon* (2015) on fair use and *malum prohibitum*, *Joaquin* (1999) and *Pearl & Dean* (2003) on the idea/expression line and the statutory nature of copyright, *Habana* (1999) on substantial copying, *Kho* (2002) on names, and *Home Cable* (2023) on infringement elements and communication to the public. In 2024 IPOPHL issued non-case "statutory fair use guidelines", which I could not read in full.

### Cited Findings
- *Republic v. Heirs of Tupaz*, G.R. No. 197335, 7 Sept 2020: restates 178.3 and 178.4 ("the copyright of a commissioned work generally belongs to the creator. However, the parties may agree in writing to transfer the copyright"); holds that an idea-contributor is not an author; and holds that an unpaid volunteer's work is neither a commissioned nor an employee work — [SC E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67242)
- *ABS-CBN Corp. v. Gozon*, G.R. No. 195956, 11 Mar 2015: the four-factor test, the transformative test, "commercial use ... can be weighed against fair use", fair use as "a matter of defense", and infringement as *malum prohibitum* — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2015/mar2015/gr_195956_2015.html)
- *Joaquin v. Drilon*, G.R. No. 108946, 28 Jan 1999: formats and concepts are unprotected; copyright is "purely a statutory right" — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1999/jan1999/gr_108946_1999.html)
- *Pearl & Dean v. Shoemart*, G.R. No. 148222, 15 Aug 2003: copyright "can cover only the works falling within the statutory enumeration or description"; copyright in drawings does not reach the objects they depict — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2003/aug2003/gr_148222_2003.html)
- *Habana v. Robles*, G.R. No. 131522, 19 July 1999: infringement does not require copying the whole; a "substantial" and "injurious" taking suffices. The Court held: "We believe that respondent Robles' act of lifting from the book of petitioners substantial portions of discussions and examples, and her failure to acknowledge the same in her book is an infringement of petitioners' copyrights." The CA decision was "SET ASIDE" and the case remanded to determine damages; Davide, C.J., dissented. — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1999/jul1999/gr_131522_1999.html)
- *Kho v. CA*, G.R. No. 115758, 19 Mar 2002: names and containers are trademark, not copyright, subjects — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2002/mar2002/gr_115758_2002.html)
- *Philippine Home Cable Holdings v. FILSCAP*, G.R. No. 188933, 21 Feb 2023 (En Banc): the two elements of infringement (ownership, plus exercise of an economic right without consent), and "communication to the public" as making a work accessible "from a place or time individually chosen" — [ChanRobles](https://chanrobles.com/cralaw/2023februarydecisions.php?id=137)
- IPOPHL "statutory fair use guidelines" (March 2024): Asia IP reports they address Sec. 184 activities and the general concept in Sec. 185. No number was given, and the binding status was not stated — [Asia IP](https://asiaiplaw.com/section/news-analysis/ipophl-releases-statutory-fair-use-guidelines-to-clarify-rules-on-copyright-exceptions)
- IPOPHL Copyright FAQ: the page did not render, so its content is **not verified** — [IPOPHL](https://www.ipophil.gov.ph/help-and-support/copyright/)

### Inferences
- On this record a Philippine court would decide a portfolio dispute by applying *Tupaz* to ownership, *Gozon*'s four factors to fair use, and *Joaquin*/*Habana* to replicas, drawing on US and EU reasoning as persuasive. Outcomes for published-app screenshots are genuinely uncertain. Outcomes for unpublished internal apps and copied internal diagrams lean toward infringement.

### Gaps
- I found no IPOPHL Bureau of Legal Affairs or Office of the Director General decision on employee software ownership or screenshots. The IPOPHL guideline and FAQ texts were not reviewed.
- I did not search Court of Appeals decisions, which are not well indexed.

## 8. International comparators: US work made for hire and fair use, EU Software Directive Art. 2(3), UK CDPA s.11(2), Berne Art. 6bis

### Takeaway
- **Employee works:** the US, EU and UK all vest employee works in the employer by default, as the Philippines does. Only the US treats the employer as the legal **author**.
- **Commissioned work:** for a contractor's software, the US default matches the Philippines: the creator owns unless there is a written assignment, because software is not on the §101 list of commissionable work-made-for-hire categories.
- **Moral rights:** the Philippines is markedly *more* protective of developers. The UK denies a right of identification for computer programs and employee works. US moral rights (VARA) cover only limited-edition "works of visual art" and exclude works made for hire. Berne 6bis sets the floor the Philippines implements in Sec. 193.
- **Fair use:** the US §107 factors are worded essentially as in Philippine Sec. 185, so US fair-use reasoning transfers readily.

### Cited Findings
- **US, 17 U.S.C. §101:** "A 'work made for hire' is— (1) a work prepared by an employee within the scope of his or her employment; or (2) a work specially ordered or commissioned for use as a contribution to a collective work, as a part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work, as a translation, as a supplementary work, as a compilation, as an instructional text, as a test, as answer material for a test, or as an atlas, if the parties expressly agree in a written instrument signed by them that the work shall be considered a work made for hire." — [17 U.S.C. §101, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/101)
- **US, 17 U.S.C. §201(b):** "In the case of a work made for hire, the employer or other person for whom the work was prepared is considered the author for purposes of this title, and, unless the parties have expressly agreed otherwise in a written instrument signed by them, owns all of the rights comprised in the copyright." — [17 U.S.C. §201, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/201)
- **US, 17 U.S.C. §107:** "the fair use of a copyrighted work ... for purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching (including multiple copies for classroom use), scholarship, or research, is not an infringement of copyright." The factors are: "(1) the purpose and character of the use, including whether such use is of a commercial nature or is for nonprofit educational purposes; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. The fact that a work is unpublished shall not itself bar a finding of fair use..." — [17 U.S.C. §107, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/107)
- **US moral rights are limited:** §106A grants attribution and integrity rights only to "the author of a work of visual art". Under §101, "A work of visual art does not include— (A)(i) any poster, map, globe, chart, technical drawing, diagram, model, applied art, ... electronic publication ...; (B) any work made for hire" — [17 U.S.C. §106A, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/106A); [17 U.S.C. §101, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/101)
- **US portfolio-display authority:** no appellate holding found. The closest are *Neri v. Monroe* (W.D. Wis. 2014, via secondary summary) and practitioner opinion (Graphic Artists Guild) — see Q4 — [CDAS](https://cdas.com/photo-glass-sculpture-integrated-ceiling-fair-use/); [Graphic Artists Guild](https://graphicartistsguild.org/fair-use-or-infringement/)
- **EU, Directive 2009/24/EC Art. 2(3):** "Where a computer program is created by an employee in the execution of his duties or following the instructions given by his employer, the employer exclusively shall be entitled to exercise all economic rights in the program so created, unless otherwise provided by contract." Art. 2(1): "The author of a computer program shall be the natural person or group of natural persons who has created the program or, where the legislation of the Member State permits, the legal person designated as the rightholder by that legislation." Art. 1(3): a program is protected "if it is original in the sense that it is the author's own intellectual creation." — [Art. 2, legislation.gov.uk EU text archive](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2009/24/article/2); [Art. 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2009/24/article/1)
- **EU, GUI and linking:** *BSA* (C-393/09) holds that a GUI is not a "computer program" but can be a work if it is the author's own intellectual creation. *Svensson* (C-466/12) and *GS Media* (C-160/15) are covered in Q5 and Q6 — [BSA](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/62009CJ0393); [Svensson](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/62012CJ0466); [GS Media](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/62015CJ0160)
- **UK, CDPA 1988 s.11(2):** "Where a literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work, or a film, is made by an employee in the course of his employment, his employer is the first owner of any copyright in the work subject to any agreement to the contrary." The words "or a film" were inserted by amendment. — [CDPA s.11](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/11)
- **UK, CDPA s.79:** "(2) The right does not apply in relation to the following descriptions of work— (a) a computer program; ... (3) The right does not apply to anything done by or with the authority of the copyright owner where copyright in the work originally vested in the author's or director's employer by virtue of section 11(2) (works produced in the course of employment)." s.81(2), on the integrity right: "The right does not apply to a computer program or to any computer-generated work." — [CDPA s.79](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/79); [CDPA s.81](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/81)
- **Berne Art. 6bis(1)**, the right "to claim authorship of the work ... even after the transfer" of economic rights, is quoted in Q3 — [LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/treaties/berne/6bis.html)

### Inferences
- **Employee developer comparison.**
  - In all four systems the employer controls the economic rights in software written in the course of employment. The Philippine formulation uses "regularly-assigned duties"; the US uses "scope of employment"; the EU says "execution of his duties or following the instructions"; the UK says "course of employment".
  - The Philippine 178.3(a) carve-out is unusually explicit: non-duty works stay with the employee "even if the employee uses the time, facilities and materials of the employer".
- **Contractor comparison.**
  - The Philippine default (178.4: the creator keeps copyright absent a written stipulation) is functionally similar to the US position for software, since software is not a §101(2) category and needs a written assignment.
  - The UK s.11(2) and EU Art. 2(3) employee rules do not reach contractors.
- **Attribution comparison.**
  - A Philippine developer has a statutory, non-assignable, perpetual attribution right that can be waived only in writing (or deemed waived for collective works).
  - A UK developer has *no* identification right in a computer program (s.79(2)(a)), nor in employee works vis-à-vis the employer (s.79(3)).
  - A US developer has none under VARA, because software and diagrams are not "works of visual art" and works made for hire are excluded.
  - EU Software Directive Arts. 1–2 do not address moral rights, which are left to national law.
- **For the portfolio:** US-style reasoning (*Neri*; practitioner consensus that portfolio display is usually fair use and rarely challenged) is persuasive in the Philippines because Sec. 185 copies §107's factors. *Gozon*'s "must fall under those listed" and "commercial use ... weighed against" glosses make the Philippine first factor potentially stricter than current US practice.

### Gaps
- *CCNV v. Reid* (1989), the US test for who is an "employee", could not be fetched (Justia 403) and is not cited.
- I did not survey EU member-state moral-rights limits for software (for example France and Germany), or national rules on employee moral rights.
- I found no US appellate decision specifically on an employee's or contractor's portfolio display of an employer's or client's software.
