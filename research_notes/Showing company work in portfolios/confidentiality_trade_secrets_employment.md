## 1. Revised Penal Code Art. 291 (revealing secrets with abuse of office) and Art. 292 (revelation of industrial secrets): text, elements, penalties after RA 10951, court application

### Takeaway
Two Revised Penal Code (RPC) articles make it a crime to reveal an employer's secrets. Art. 291 covers any "manager, employee, or servant" who learns the principal's secrets "in such capacity" and reveals them. Art. 292 covers staff of a "manufacturing or industrial establishment" who reveal "secrets of the industry" to the owner's prejudice. RA 10951 (2017) raised the maximum fine for both to PHP 100,000 and left the jail terms unchanged. No reported Supreme Court decision applying either article was found, so how far they reach a software company, a former employee or a portfolio disclosure is untested.

### Cited Findings
- [STATUTE] Original Art. 291: "Revealing secrets with abuse of office.-The penalty of arresto mayor and a fine not exceeding 500 pesos shall be imposed upon any manager, employee, or servant who, in such capacity, shall learn the secrets of his principal or master and shall reveal such secrets." — [Revised Penal Code, Act No. 3815 (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/acts/act1930/act_3815_1930.html)
- [STATUTE] Original Art. 292: "Revelation of industrial secrets.-The penalty of prision correccional in its minimum and medium periods and fine not exceeding 500 pesos shall be imposed upon the person in charge, employee or workman of any manufacturing or industrial establishment who, to the prejudice of the owner thereof, shall reveal the secrets of the industry of the latter." — [RPC (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/acts/act1930/act_3815_1930.html)
- [STATUTE] Both articles sit in Chapter Three, "Discovery and Revelation of Secrets", next to Art. 290 (discovering secrets through seizure of correspondence). The chapter comes immediately before Title Ten (Crimes Against Property). — [RPC (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/acts/act1930/act_3815_1930.html)
- [STATUTE] RA 10951, Sec. 77, amends Art. 291 to read: "The penalty of arresto mayor and a fine not exceeding One hundred thousand pesos (₱100,000) shall be imposed upon any manager, employee or servant who, in such capacity, shall learn the secrets of his principal or master and shall reveal such secrets." — [RA 10951 (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2017/ra_10951_2017.html)
- [STATUTE] RA 10951, Sec. 78, amends Art. 292 to read: "The penalty of prisión correccional in its minimum and medium periods and a fine not exceeding One hundred thousand pesos (₱100,000) shall be imposed upon the person in charge, employee or workman of any manufacturing or industrial establishment who, to the prejudice of the owner thereof, shall reveal the secrets of the industry of the latter." — [RA 10951](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2017/ra_10951_2017.html)
- [STATUTE] RA 10951 was approved on August 29, 2017. Under Sec. 102 it took effect "within fifteen (15) days after its publication in at least two (2) newspapers of general circulation." — [RA 10951](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2017/ra_10951_2017.html)

- [SECONDARY] Practitioner commentary lists the Art. 292 elements as: (1) the offender is a person in charge, employee or workman of a manufacturing or industrial establishment; (2) there is a secret of the industry belonging to the owner; (3) the offender reveals it; (4) the revelation prejudices the owner. - Targeted searches for Supreme Court decisions applying Art. 291 or Art. 292 found none (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- **Elements, read from the text.** Art. 291: (1) the offender is a manager, employee or servant; (2) he learned the secrets of his principal or master in that capacity; (3) he revealed them. Unlike Art. 292, the text has no "prejudice" element. Art. 292: (1) person in charge, employee or workman of a manufacturing or industrial establishment; (2) a secret "of the industry" of the owner; (3) revelation; (4) prejudice to the owner.
- **Art. 292 probably fits a software house poorly.** Its wording ("manufacturing or industrial establishment", "secrets of the industry") targets factories and production processes. For a developer, Art. 291 is the more plausible criminal provision. Neither point has been tested in court.
- **"Secrets" is not defined in the RPC.** Information that is already public is hard to call a "secret": the existence of a published app, its store screenshots, or an announced fact that DGSI built an app for an LGU. The categories at risk are internal architecture, security design, unreleased features, the internals of the privately distributed responder app, and metrics that have not been published.
- **Former employees.** Art. 291 requires the secret to have been learned "in such capacity", meaning during employment. It does not say the revelation must also happen during employment, so revealing after leaving a secret learned on the job is arguably covered. No case was found either way.
- **Contractors.** Art. 291 names a "manager, employee, or servant". Since penal statutes are construed strictly, an independent contractor may fall outside it. This is untested.
- **Website disclosure.** RA 10175 Sec. 6 could raise the penalty by one degree when the revelation is made through ICT, such as a portfolio website. No case was found applying Sec. 6 to Art. 291 or 292.
- **Overall risk.** For a portfolio, a criminal case is a lower practical risk than contract, civil or labour consequences. It is not zero for material that is clearly secret.

### Gaps
- No reported Supreme Court decision applying Art. 291 or 292 was found, so how courts read "secrets", "in such capacity" and "manufacturing or industrial establishment" is not verified. The standard commentary (L.B. Reyes, *The Revised Penal Code*, Book Two) is not online and was not consulted.
- RPC Art. 71 (graduated scale) and Art. 76 (table of periods) were not opened. The common figure for the minimum and medium periods of prision correccional (6 months and 1 day to 4 years and 2 months) and the exact effect of a one-degree increase are not verified.

## 2. Is there a dedicated Philippine trade-secrets statute, and how is "undisclosed information" protected (IP Code Sec. 4.1(g), TRIPS Art. 39, Civil Code Arts. 19, 20, 21, 28, contract)? Pending bills

### Takeaway
No dedicated trade-secrets statute was found as of 2026-10-23. The IP Code lists "Protection of Undisclosed Information (n, TRIPS)" as an intellectual property right in Sec. 4.1(g), but no other provision of the Code deals with it. Protection instead comes from several sources:
- the TRIPS Art. 39 treaty obligation;
- Supreme Court doctrine: *Pennswell* holds that trade secrets are proprietary rights and that courts ordinarily enjoin disclosure by someone who received the information in confidence;
- RPC Arts. 291 and 292;
- Civil Code Arts. 19, 20, 21 and 28;
- statutes for particular sectors;
- contract (Civil Code Arts. 1159 and 1306).

No trade-secret bill was located. IPOPHL's June 2026 statement on pending IP Code reforms does not mention trade secrets, and no reform found has become law.

### Cited Findings

- [STATUTE] A full-text search of the lawphil copy of RA 8293 found "Undisclosed Information" only in Sec. 4.1(g). The phrase "trade secret" does not appear anywhere in it. — [RA 8293](https://lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra1997/ra_8293_1997.html)
- [STATUTE] RA 10372, approved Feb. 28, 2013, is the main amending law. - [TREATY] TRIPS Art. 39.1: "In the course of ensuring effective protection against unfair competition as provided in Article 10bis of the Paris Convention (1967), Members shall protect undisclosed information in accordance with paragraph 2 and data submitted to governments or governmental agencies in accordance with paragraph 3." — [WTO TRIPS text](https://www.wto.org/english/docs_e/legal_e/27-trips_04d_e.htm)
- [TREATY] TRIPS Art. 39.2: "Natural and legal persons shall have the possibility of preventing information lawfully within their control from being disclosed to, acquired by, or used by others without their consent in a manner contrary to honest commercial practices so long as such information: (a) is secret in the sense that it is not, as a body or in the precise configuration and assembly of its components, generally known among or readily accessible to persons within the circles that normally deal with the kind of information in question; (b) has commercial value because it is secret; and (c) has been subject to reasonable steps under the circumstances, by the person lawfully in control of the information, to keep it secret." — [WTO](https://www.wto.org/english/docs_e/legal_e/27-trips_04d_e.htm)
- [TREATY] TRIPS footnote 10: "'a manner contrary to honest commercial practices' shall mean at least practices such as breach of contract, breach of confidence and inducement to breach, and includes the acquisition of undisclosed information by third parties who knew, or were grossly negligent in failing to know, that such practices were involved in the acquisition." — [WTO](https://www.wto.org/english/docs_e/legal_e/27-trips_04d_e.htm)
- [CASE] *Pennswell* (2007), definition: "A trade secret is defined as a plan or process, tool, mechanism or compound known only to its owner and those of his employees to whom it is necessary to confide it." It continues: "A trade secret may consist of any formula, pattern, device, or compilation of information that: (1) is used in one's business; and (2) gives the employer an opportunity to obtain an advantage over competitors who do not possess the information. Generally, a trade secret is a process or device intended for continuous use in the operation of the business, for example, a machine or formula, but can be a price list or catalogue or specialized customer list. It is indubitable that trade secrets constitute proprietary rights. The inventor, discoverer, or possessor of a trade secret or similar innovation has rights therein which may be treated as property, and ordinarily an injunction will be granted to prevent the disclosure of the trade secret by one who obtained the information 'in confidence' or through a 'confidential relationship.'" — [Pennswell](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2007/dec2007/gr_172835_2007.html)
- [CASE] *Pennswell* adopted six factors from American jurisprudence (its footnote traces them to the Uniform Trade Secrets Act): "(1) the extent to which the information is known outside of the employer's business; (2) the extent to which the information is known by employees and others involved in the business; (3) the extent of measures taken by the employer to guard the secrecy of the information; (4) the value of the information to the employer and to competitors; (5) the amount of effort or money expended by the company in developing the information; and (6) the extent to which the information could be easily or readily obtained through an independent source." — [Pennswell](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2007/dec2007/gr_172835_2007.html)

### Inferences
- **How a trade-secret case is actually brought in the Philippines.** Through some mix of:
  - breach of an NDA or employment contract (Arts. 1159 and 1170);
  - abuse of rights or acts contrary to good customs (Arts. 19 to 21);
  - Art. 28, where competition involves deceit or machination;
  - an injunction, relying on *Pennswell*'s statement that courts ordinarily enjoin disclosure by someone who got the information in confidence;
  - criminally, RPC Art. 291 or 292.

  Sec. 4.1(g) of the IP Code states a policy but creates no cause of action by itself.
- **What fails the test.** *Pennswell*'s factors (1) and (3) track TRIPS (a) and (c): how widely the information is known outside, and what measures were taken to keep it secret. Information DGSI publishes itself (store listings, press releases, LGU announcements) or never guarded is unlikely to be a trade secret. Internal architecture or security design kept within the team probably is.
- **Pending bills are not law.** Any trade-secret bill or IP Code amendment would bind no one until enacted and published.

### Gaps
- The Senate LIS and House bill databases could not be searched directly, so the absence of a trade-secret bill in the 20th Congress (2025 to 2028) is not confirmed.
- No IPOPHL guidance specifically on undisclosed information or trade secrets was found.
- No Philippine decision was found granting an injunction or damages for misuse of software trade secrets specifically.

## 3. Labor Code Art. 297 (formerly 282): is leaking confidential information or breaching a confidentiality policy a just cause for dismissal? Supreme Court decisions

### Takeaway
Yes. Leaking confidential information can justify dismissal as serious misconduct, willful breach of trust (Art. 297(a) and (c)) or an analogous cause. But the Supreme Court requires all of the following:
- proof of an actual disclosure of information that is genuinely confidential;
- wilfulness, not mere carelessness;
- for loss of trust and confidence, that the employee holds a position of trust;
- a clear and reasonable company rule;
- a penalty proportionate to the offence.

*Gana* (2008) upheld the dismissal of a manager who e-mailed internal operational information to a business partner. *Austria* (1999), *Simbillo* (2016) and *Yonzon* (2021) struck down dismissals for one or more of these reasons: no proof, no actual leak, carelessness rather than wilfulness, rank-and-file status, or a vague "confidential information" rule. These rules only matter while someone is employed. A former employee faces civil or criminal exposure instead.

### Cited Findings
- [STATUTE] Labor Code text, shown on lawphil as pre-renumbering Art. 282: "An employer may terminate an employment for any of the following causes: Serious misconduct or willful disobedience by the employee of the lawful orders of his employer or representative in connection with his work; Gross and habitual neglect by the employee of his duties; Fraud or willful breach by the employee of the trust reposed in him by his employer or duly authorized representative; Commission of a crime or offense by the employee against the person of his employer or any immediate member of his family or his duly authorized representatives; and Other causes analogous to the foregoing." — [Labor Code, PD 442 (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/statutes/presdecs/pd1974/pd_442_1974.html)
- [CASE] Renumbering: *Fabricator Philippines, Inc. v. Estolas*, G.R. Nos. 224308-09, Sept. 27, 2017 (Second Division, Perlas-Bernabe, J.) refers to "Article 297 (formerly Article 282)". Its footnote reads: "See Department of Labor and Employment Department Advisory No. 01, Series of 2015, entitled 'RENUMBERING OF THE LABOR CODE OF THE PHILIPPINES, AS AMENDED.' See also Section 5 of Republic Act No. 10151". — [SC E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/4/63466)
- [CASE] *Fabricator* on serious misconduct: misconduct is "a transgression of some established and definite rule of action, a forbidden act, a dereliction of duty, willful in character, and implies wrongful intent and not mere error in judgment." The elements that must concur: "the misconduct must be serious; ... it must relate to the performance of the employee's duties, showing that the employee has become unfit to continue working for the employer; and ... it must have been performed with wrongful intent." — [SC E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/4/63466)
- [CASE] *Yonzon v. Coca-Cola Bottlers Philippines, Inc.*, G.R. No. 226244, June 16, 2021 (First Division, Carandang, J.), facts: an HR Administration Analyst disclosed co-employees' salaries in an NLRC pleading. She was dismissed for "unauthorized disclosure of confidential company information to third parties and loss of trust and confidence."

  - There are two classes of trust positions: managerial employees, and fiduciary rank-and-file employees "who in the normal and routine exercise of their functions regularly handle significant amounts of money or property." She was in neither class.
  - The company rule (covering "other company classified/restricted/confidential information") "is unfair and unreasonable. It suffers from vagueness." The Court added: "Coca-Cola did not give any examples or standards on how an information may be considered as confidential/classified/restricted leaving the same subject to the company's whims and caprices."
  - She was held illegally dismissed and awarded separation pay instead of reinstatement, plus full backwages.

  — [SC E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/4/67790)
- [CASE] *Yonzon*, on management prerogative: "While the adoption and enforcement of the provisions of the Red Book is a valid exercise of Coca-Cola's management prerogative, such exercise is not absolute and unbridled. An employer must ensure that its rules and regulations on work-related activities of the employees must be fair and reasonable and the corresponding penalties, when prescribed is commensurate to the offense involved and to the degree of the infraction." — [SC E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/4/67790)
- [CASE] *Interadent Zahntechnik Philippines, Inc. v. Simbillo*, G.R. No. 207315, Nov. 23, 2016 (Second Division, Del Castillo, J.), facts: a finance and accounting manager who was also treasurer was dismissed over a Facebook post said to reveal a BIR matter.
  - The standard: "loss of trust and confidence must be based on willful breach of trust, that is, done intentionally, knowingly and purposely, without justifiable excuse, as distinguished from an act done carelessly, thoughtlessly, heedlessly, or inadvertently."
  - On the post: "the Facebook entry did not contain any corporate record or any confidential information. Otherwise stated, there was really no actual leakage of information." It also "did not mention any specific name of employer/company/government agency or person."
  - On penalty: "A lighter penalty would have sufficed for Simbillo's laxity and carelessness. As this Court has held, termination of employment is a drastic measure reserved for the most serious of offenses."
  - The Court of Appeals' finding of illegal dismissal was affirmed.

  — [lawphil](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2016/nov2016/gr_207315_2016.html); [SC E-Library](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62653)
- [CASE] *Gana v. NLRC*, G.R. No. 164640, June 13, 2008 (Third Division, Austria-Martinez, J.), facts: a manager sent e-mails to an official of the business partner Trans-America "divulging various confidential information about the business operations and transactions". - [CASE] *Austria v. NLRC*, G.R. No. 123646, July 14, 1999 (Second Division, Bellosillo, J.), facts: a Credit and Collection Manager had signed a "Confidentiality Agreement" promising "not to disclose to anyone outside the company any technical, operational and other such information acquired in the course of his employment, unless otherwise duly authorized by private respondent, on pain of immediate dismissal". He was dismissed for allegedly disclosing confidential supply-source information to prospective competitors.
  - The Supreme Court held: "while loss of confidence or breach of trust is a valid ground for termination, it must rest on some basis which must be convincingly established. An employee may not be dismissed on mere presumptions and supposition..."

- [CASE] *Duncan Association of Detailman-PTGWO v. Glaxo Wellcome Philippines, Inc.*, G.R. No. 162994, Sept. 17, 2004 (Second Division, Tinga, J.) upheld a conflict-of-interest policy: "Glaxo has a right to guard its trade secrets, manufacturing formulas, marketing strategies and other confidential programs and information from competitors, especially so that it and Astra are rival companies in the highly competitive pharmaceutical industry." *Pennswell* later cited it as evidence that "our cases on labor are replete with examples of a protectionist stance towards the trade secrets of employers." — [Duncan (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2004/sep2004/gr_162994_2004.html); [Pennswell](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2007/dec2007/gr_172835_2007.html)

### Inferences
- **Rank-and-file developers.** After *Yonzon*, loss of trust and confidence is contestable for a rank-and-file developer, unless the developer counts as a fiduciary rank-and-file employee who handles significant "property". None of the cases found decides whether access to source code or systems counts as handling property. The likelier grounds would be serious misconduct, or willful disobedience of a clear, reasonable and known policy such as "do not publish internal diagrams".
- **Mapping the portfolio onto the cases.** Knowingly publishing internal architecture diagrams or unpublished metrics looks more like *Gana*: deliberate disclosure outside the company. A portfolio limited to public material (store links, public screenshots) looks more like *Simbillo* and *Yonzon*, where no confidential information came out. Dismissal would be hard to sustain there, especially under a vague policy.
- **Handbook rules need specificity.** Under *Yonzon*, a generic rule like "all company information is confidential" is weak support for a dismissal, although it may still operate as a contractual promise.
- **Former employees.** A just-cause dismissal is irrelevant once employment has ended. For a former employee the exposure becomes a civil suit in the regular courts (*Dai-Chi*, Q4) and possibly RPC Art. 291.
- **Procedure.** Procedural due process (the two-notice rule) also applies to any dismissal. It was not researched here.

### Gaps
- No Supreme Court decision was found on an IT or software employee publicly posting the employer's system details (portfolio, GitHub, blog).
- DOLE Department Order No. 147-15 (rules on just and authorized causes) was not opened; not verified.
- The text of DOLE Department Advisory No. 01, s. 2015 was not opened. The renumbering is verified only through the *Fabricator* footnote.

## 4. Contract law (Civil Code Arts. 1159 and 1306): enforceability of NDAs, IP-assignment and non-compete clauses; do confidentiality duties survive the end of employment?

### Takeaway
A contract stipulation binds the parties as law (Art. 1159) as long as it is not contrary to law, morals, good customs, public order or public policy (Art. 1306). Post-employment non-competes are valid only if reasonable as to time, trade and place, and the employer must prove reasonableness (*Rivera*, 2006). *Tiu* (2007) upheld a two-year pre-need ban, partly because the employee had been privy to confidential strategies. Confidentiality clauses restrain an employee less than non-competes do, and no Supreme Court decision found rejects a reasonable post-employment NDA. A signed NDA stated to survive termination would therefore likely bind. Damages claims on post-employment covenants belong in the regular courts (*Dai-Chi*, 1994). Without any contract, a former employee's residual duties come from RPC Art. 291, Civil Code Arts. 19 to 21, and the injunction *Pennswell* describes against someone who received information in confidence.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- **Survival clauses.** A confidentiality clause stated to survive termination binds a former employee under Art. 1159, subject to Art. 1306 reasonableness. An NDA does not stop anyone from working, so *Rivera*'s restraint-of-trade concerns apply only weakly. The realistic attacks are overbreadth or vagueness ("all information whatsoever, forever") under Arts. 1306, 1377 and 1702, and the point that public information cannot sensibly be "confidential".
- **No signed clause.** No Philippine statute imposes a general civil-law duty of confidence after employment. Real secrets still have residual protection: Arts. 19 and 21 (acting against good faith or good customs and causing damage), *Pennswell*'s confidential-relationship injunction, and RPC Art. 291. Information that is not secret, such as a public app listing, carries no duty of silence.
- **Liquidated damages.** Liquidated-damages clauses in NDAs are enforceable but can be reduced if iniquitous (Art. 1229). *Tiu* shows the Court may refuse to reduce them where there was bad faith.
- **Non-competes.** These do not govern portfolio display as such. They become relevant because the portfolio has a Services page: offering LGU public-safety app work, or approaching DGSI's LGU clients, could breach a non-compete or non-solicitation clause if one exists and is reasonable under *Tiu*/*Rivera*.

## 5. Typical clauses in Philippine tech employment contracts and handbooks (confidentiality, IP assignment, publicity/social media/portfolio) and their interaction with IP Code Sec. 178.3(a)

### Takeaway
Supreme Court decisions show the clause types Philippine employers actually use: confidentiality agreements covering "any technical, operational and other such information acquired in the course of his employment", handbook non-disclosure rules, conflict-of-interest clauses, and two-year non-involvement clauses. No authoritative Philippine source was found on "portfolio/publicity" clauses specifically.

The statutory default ownership rules are:
- **Works or inventions from "regularly-assigned duties":** the employer owns the copyright or patent (IP Code Secs. 178.3(b) and 30.2(b)).
- **Works outside regular duties:** the employee owns them "even if the employee uses the time, facilities and materials of the employer" (Sec. 178.3(a)).
- **Commissioned works:** the commissioning party owns "the work", but the copyright stays with the creator unless there is a written stipulation (Sec. 178.4).
- **Assignments:** copyright needs a "written indication" before it counts as assigned or licensed (Sec. 180.2, as amended).

Copyright ownership and confidentiality are separate questions. Owning, or not owning, the code does not decide whether facts about it may be disclosed.

### Cited Findings
- [CONTRACT, quoted in CASE] Coca-Cola's "Red Book", Rule 3, Sec. 31 punishes "Giving of, supplying, and disclosing to the unauthorized person or the competitors, classified trade secrets, and other confidential materials, information, data or documents relating to the Company's operations, programs formulae, processes, market studies, surveys, and other Company classified/restricted/confidential information, or other data, documents information similar to those enumerated herein." The company's Code of Business Ethics, rule 14, imposes an "Obligation not to disclose confidential information regarding processes, methods, strategies, plans, pro jects [sic], technical or market data, or information of any kind". - [CONTRACT, quoted in CASE] PHILSTEEL "Confidentiality Agreement" (1987): the employee "agreed not to disclose to anyone outside the company any technical, operational and other such information acquired in the course of his employment, unless otherwise duly authorized by private respondent, on pain of immediate dismissal." — [Austria v. NLRC (SC E-Library)](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37251)
- [CONTRACT, quoted in CASE] Glaxo's conflict-of-interest policy requires employees "To avoid having personal or family interest, financial or otherwise, in any competitor supplier or other businesses which may consciously or unconsciously influence their actions or decisions and thus deprive Glaxo Wellcome of legitimate profit." — [Duncan (lawphil)](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2004/sep2004/gr_162994_2004.html)

### Inferences
How the rules play out in each situation the brief describes:
- **Employee, work within regular duties** (the DGSI LGU apps, the responder app, Eventbook, and the push-to-talk prototype if it was assigned work):
  - By default the employer owns the copyright and any inventions (Secs. 178.3(b) and 30.2(b)).
  - Whether details can be shown depends on the contract or policy and on whether the information is secret.
  - The employee can still describe their role and skills.
  - Sec. 193.1 gives the author an attribution right that is independent of the economic rights. It is untested whether an employee-author can rely on it to display employer-owned work, and it does not override confidentiality obligations.
- **Employee, side project outside regular duties:**
  - By default the employee owns the copyright, even if the project was built on company time or equipment (Sec. 178.3(a)).
  - An "all works created during employment" clause can transfer it only as a written assignment (Sec. 180.2). A signed contract clause would probably meet that writing requirement.
  - Overbroad clauses, for example covering off-duty work unrelated to the business, are open to challenge under Arts. 1306, 1702 and 1377. This is untested.
  - Embedding the employer's confidential information in a side project is still a confidentiality problem, whoever owns the copyright.
- **Contractor or commissioned work:**
  - The paying client owns "the work", but the copyright stays with the creator unless there is a written stipulation (Sec. 178.4).
  - Confidentiality duties come mainly from the services contract or NDA and from Civil Code Arts. 19 to 21.
  - RPC Art. 291 may not apply, since a contractor is arguably not a "manager, employee, or servant".
- **Current employee:** exposed to Labor Code discipline (Q3), contract liability, civil liability and possibly RPC Art. 291.
- **Former employee:** no dismissal risk. The surviving exposure is a survival clause in the contract, civil liability (Arts. 19 to 21, injunction) and RPC Art. 291 for secrets learned while employed. Claims go to the regular courts (*Dai-Chi*).
- **With an NDA:** the categories the NDA defines control. If it lists architecture, metrics, client information or unreleased features, showing them is a breach even if they would fail the strict trade-secret test (Art. 1159), subject to reasonableness and to the vagueness concern in *Yonzon*. Any publicity or portfolio clause would govern use of company and client names and screenshots.
- **Without an NDA:** only real secrets (the *Pennswell*/TRIPS test) and information received in confidence are protected. Public information is free to show as far as confidentiality goes; the copyright and trademark rules covered elsewhere still apply.
- **Practical safeguard:** the clearest protection is written permission (an express contract clause or e-mail consent) from DGSI, and from the LGU client where its brand or data is involved, covering the named items.

- No Philippine primary or authoritative secondary source was found describing typical "publicity", "social media" or "portfolio" clauses in Philippine tech contracts. What such clauses usually say is not verified.
- Whether the user signed any of these clauses is unknown (per the brief).
- No Supreme Court decision applying Sec. 178.3(a) to an employee's side project was found.
- Sec. 195 (waiver of moral rights) and Sec. 198 (term of moral rights, amended by RA 10372) were not opened; not verified.

## 6. What counts as confidential or a trade secret in software, versus what is not protectable (public information, an employee's general skill and knowledge)

### Takeaway
**Likely protectable:** non-public information that is valuable because it is secret and that the company guards. That includes internal architecture and data flows, internal APIs, security design, source code, unreleased features and roadmaps, unpublished usage or performance metrics, client pricing, and compilations such as client or vendor lists. *Pennswell* expressly mentions a "compilation of information" and a "price list or catalogue or specialized customer list". The DTSA definition lists "plans, compilations, program devices, ... designs, prototypes, methods, techniques, processes, procedures, programs, or codes".

**Not protectable:** information that is generally known or readily accessible, such as published store listings, public screenshots and public announcements, and an employee's general skill, knowledge and experience. The EU Directive carves out skills expressly, and US law limits injunctions based "merely on the information the person knows". No Philippine case on that point was found; for the Philippines it is inferred from *Pennswell*'s factors and the reasonableness doctrine in *Rivera*/*Tiu*.

For disciplinary rules, Philippine employers must also define confidential categories with some specificity (*Yonzon*).

- [COMPARATOR] 18 U.S.C. 1839(3): "the term 'trade secret' means all forms and types of financial, business, scientific, technical, economic, or engineering information, including patterns, plans, compilations, program devices, formulas, designs, prototypes, methods, techniques, processes, procedures, programs, or codes, whether tangible or intangible, and whether or how stored, compiled, or memorialized physically, electronically, graphically, photographically, or in writing if— (A) the owner thereof has taken reasonable measures to keep such information secret; and (B) the information derives independent economic value, actual or potential, from not being generally known to, and not being readily ascertainable through proper means by, another person who can obtain economic value from the disclosure or use of the information". — [Cornell LII, 18 U.S.C. 1839](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1839)

- **Company, client and LGU names, app names, Google Play / App Store links.** Low confidentiality risk where the apps are publicly listed (white-label apps under LGU brands with com.dgsi.* package names). The app's existence and DGSI's role are then public. Risk arises only if a client relationship has not been made public, or if a contract or NDA forbids naming clients. - **Official store screenshots and app icons.** Already public, so not confidential. Re-hosting them raises copyright, trademark and publicity questions (other researchers) and may fall under a publicity clause.
- **Self-made "replica" screens with fictional data.** No confidentiality problem with the data, since it is invented. - **LGU brand colours.** Already public in the published apps; low confidentiality risk (trademark aside).
- **System architecture diagrams showing how the parts connect and who built each part.** The highest-risk item.
  - Internal architecture, data flows, internal APIs, infrastructure and vendor choices, and security design are classic non-public technical information. They match the DTSA's "designs ... methods, techniques, processes, procedures, programs" and *Pennswell*'s "plan or process".
  - For LGU public-safety and emergency systems, disclosure also creates security risk, which strengthens the employer's legitimate interest.
  - Lower-risk options: a generic diagram of the user's own role and the public tech stack, without internal component names, endpoints, hosting or vendors, or security controls. Or get written approval.
- **Metrics and numbers.**
  - Figures from internal dashboards are likely confidential: active users, incident volumes, response times, revenue, and download counts beyond what the stores show.
  - Figures visible on the store listing (install ranges, ratings) are public.
  - Self-measured performance numbers about the user's own code should still be checked against any NDA.

Showing their screens, flows or internals probably breaches any confidentiality obligation in a contract or policy, and may meet the trade-secret test. A plain description carries much less risk, for example "built a responder app for LGU emergency teams (private distribution)" with no screenshots or internals.
- **Technology logos shown as skills.** These reflect general skill and knowledge, not employer confidential information. Trademark-use questions are separate.
- **Eventbook Exhibitors** (published by DGSI on both stores). Public listing; low confidentiality risk.
- **ERP, telemedicine and virtual ID work from an earlier job.** The same analysis applies against that employer. Being a former employee removes the Labor Code risk, but not contract, civil or criminal exposure.
- **The FAQ saying company code is proprietary.** Consistent with confidentiality. Not showing company code is the right call.
- **Personal projects owned outright.** Free to show, provided no employer confidential information or employer-owned code is embedded. See the Sec. 178.3(a) side-project analysis and any assignment clause in Q5.

## 7. Comparators: US Defend Trade Secrets Act (18 U.S.C. 1836, 1839(3)) and EU Trade Secrets Directive (EU) 2016/943 (Art. 2(1) definition, Art. 1(3) employees' experience and skills)

### Takeaway
The US DTSA and the EU Directive define a trade secret with essentially the same three-part test as TRIPS Art. 39: the information is secret, it has value because it is secret, and reasonable steps were taken to keep it secret. Both also protect employee mobility. DTSA injunctions may not "prevent a person from entering into an employment relationship" and must rest on "evidence of threatened misappropriation and not merely on the information the person knows". The EU Directive gives no ground for "limiting employees' use of experience and skills honestly acquired in the normal course of their employment". Neither law applies in the Philippines, but both illustrate the general-skill line Philippine courts are likely to draw under *Pennswell* and *Rivera*.

### Cited Findings
- [COMPARATOR] 18 U.S.C. 1839(3), the DTSA definition, is quoted in Q6. — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1839)
- [COMPARATOR] 18 U.S.C. 1839(5)(B)(ii)(II): misappropriation includes disclosure or use by someone who knew or had reason to know the knowledge was "acquired under circumstances giving rise to a duty to maintain the secrecy of the trade secret or limit the use of the trade secret". — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1839)
- [COMPARATOR] 18 U.S.C. 1836(b)(3)(A)(i): an injunction order must not "(I) prevent a person from entering into an employment relationship, and that conditions placed on such employment shall be based on evidence of threatened misappropriation and not merely on the information the person knows; or (II) otherwise conflict with an applicable State law prohibiting restraints on the practice of a lawful profession, trade, or business". — [Cornell LII, 18 U.S.C. 1836](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1836)

### Inferences
- In all three systems (the Philippines through *Pennswell* and TRIPS, the US and the EU), publicly available information and general skills fall outside trade-secret protection.
- A contract can extend protection to "confidential information" beyond strict trade secrets. EU Art. 1(3)(c) and DTSA Sec. 1836 then limit restraints on mobility; the Philippines reaches a similar result through Art. 1306 public policy and *Rivera*.
- A developer who shows general skills (React Native, Node, architecture patterns in the abstract) is on safe ground in all three systems. Showing employer-specific designs, internal metrics or unreleased features is not.
- Freelance clients in the US or EU may use contracts governed by their own law. Those laws do not change the developer's existing Philippine obligations to DGSI.

### Gaps
- US state law (the Uniform Trade Secrets Act as adopted by states, and state non-compete limits) and the status of any US federal non-compete rule were not researched (outside scope).
- The EUR-Lex original of Directive 2016/943 was not opened directly (legislation.gov.uk copy used).
